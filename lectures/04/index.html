Lecture 04 · Module 2 · Robotics

Worked example: kinematics, energies & Lagrangian of a 2-link planar arm

From transformation matrices to Euler–Lagrange torques — every step shown, nothing skipped.

This lecture is one long worked example. We take a textbook 2-link planar arm with point masses at the tips, and walk — slowly, deliberately, no algebraic leaps — from the homogeneous transformation matrices all the way to the joint-torque equations. The point isn't the answer; it's that every line should feel unavoidable. By the end you should be able to redo this on a blank page, and you'll have the muscle memory you'll need when we tackle full Euler–Lagrange dynamics in Module 3.

§ 0

What this lecture covers

A single, fully expanded worked example: a planar 2-link robot arm with tip masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ at the distal ends of links of length $L_1$ and $L_2$, joints at angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$.

We will, in order:

  1. Write the homogeneous transformations $T_1$ and $T_2$.
  2. Read off the Cartesian positions of the two tip masses.
  3. Differentiate to get their velocities, then square-and-sum to get $v_1^2$ and $v_2^2$.
  4. Assemble the kinetic energy $T$, the potential energy $V$, and the Lagrangian $L = T - V$.
  5. Apply Euler–Lagrange to obtain explicit expressions for the joint torques $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$.

There are no skipped steps. Every trig identity, every chain-rule application, every product-rule term is written out.

If a single line ever feels like it came out of nowhere, stop and re-derive it. That's the whole point of this lecture.

What you'll need

Comfort with $2\times 2$ rotation matrices, the chain rule, and the identities $\sin^2\alpha+\cos^2\alpha=1$ and $\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta=\cos(\alpha-\beta)$.

Why this matters

The torque equations we derive at the end are exactly the inputs Module 3 will feed into the Euler–Lagrange formulation for full robot dynamics.

§ 1

Homogeneous transformations for each link

For the planar arm we use $3\times 3$ homogeneous transformations that combine a 2D rotation with a translation.

The transformation from the base to the end of link 1 (where mass $m_1$ sits):

$$T_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_1 & -\sin\theta_1 & L_1\cos\theta_1 \\ \sin\theta_1 & \cos\theta_1 & L_1\sin\theta_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

For link 2, written in the base frame (so the translation is the full reach to mass $m_2$):

$$T_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ \sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

The rotation block carries the cumulative angle $\theta_1+\theta_2$, and the translation column gives the position of the end of link 2 in the base frame.

Why homogeneous form

Stacking rotation and translation into one $3\times 3$ matrix lets us compose link transforms by simple matrix multiplication: $T_2 = T_1 \cdot T_2^{(1)}$, with no extra bookkeeping for the offsets.

§ 2

Cartesian positions of the tip masses

The translation column of each transformation gives the $(x,y)$ position of the corresponding mass directly.

For $m_1$, at the end of link 1:

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &= L_1\cos\theta_1 \\ y_1 &= L_1\sin\theta_1 \end{aligned}$$

For $m_2$, at the end of link 2:

$$\begin{aligned} x_2 &= L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ y_2 &= L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{aligned}$$

Nothing magical — these are just the last column of $T_1$ and $T_2$ respectively.

§ 3

Velocities via the chain rule

Differentiate each position with respect to time, remembering that both joint angles are functions of time.

For $m_1$:

$$\begin{aligned} \dot{x}_1 &= -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1 \\ \dot{y}_1 &= L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1 \end{aligned}$$

For $m_2$, the inner derivative of $\theta_1+\theta_2$ is $\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2$:

$$\begin{aligned} \dot{x}_2 &= -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1 - L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \\ \dot{y}_2 &= L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \end{aligned}$$

Don't drop the $\dot\theta_2$

The most common mistake here is forgetting that the angle of link 2 in the base frame is $\theta_1+\theta_2$, so its time derivative is $\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2$, not just $\dot\theta_2$.

§ 4

Squared speeds $v_1^2$ and $v_2^2$

For $m_1$ the result collapses immediately by the Pythagorean identity:

$$v_1^2 = \dot{x}_1^2 + \dot{y}_1^2 = L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2\big(\sin^2\theta_1+\cos^2\theta_1\big) = L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2$$

For $m_2$ we expand fully — no leaps. After squaring both components and grouping:

$$\begin{aligned} v_2^2 =\;& L_1^2\sin^2\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2\sin^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 \\ &+ 2L_1L_2\sin\theta_1\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \\ &+ L_1^2\cos^2\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2\cos^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 \\ &+ 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_1\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \end{aligned}$$

Now apply $\sin^2+\cos^2 = 1$ to the two pure-square groups, and the identity $\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta = \cos(\alpha-\beta)$ to the cross terms. Since $(\theta_1+\theta_2)-\theta_1 = \theta_2$:

$$\boxed{\,v_2^2 = L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)\,}$$

That $\cos\theta_2$ — the relative angle between the two links — is the geometric heart of the whole derivation.

The key identity

$\cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta = \cos(\alpha-\beta)$. When $\alpha = \theta_1+\theta_2$ and $\beta = \theta_1$, the difference is exactly $\theta_2$ — which is why the coupling term depends on the elbow angle, not on the absolute orientation.

§ 5

Kinetic and potential energy

Kinetic energy is the sum over the two point masses:

$$T = \tfrac{1}{2} m_1 v_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} m_2 v_2^2$$

Substituting the expressions from §4:

$$T = \tfrac{1}{2} m_1 L_1^2 \dot\theta_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} m_2 \left[ L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \right]$$

Potential energy — taking $y$ upward and gravity $g$ downward, with the base as the reference:

$$V = m_1 g\, y_1 + m_2 g\, y_2 = m_1 g L_1\sin\theta_1 + m_2 g\big[L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\big]$$

Sign convention

$V$ uses $\sin\theta$ because the masses rise as the links rotate up from the horizontal. If you'd chosen $\theta$ from the vertical instead, you'd get $\cos\theta$ — same physics, different bookkeeping.

§ 6

Assembling and expanding the Lagrangian

The Lagrangian is $L = T - V$. Before differentiating, it pays to expand the kinetic energy fully so every $\dot\theta_i^2$ and $\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2$ term is visible.

First, $(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 = \dot\theta_1^2 + 2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2 + \dot\theta_2^2$, and the cross term expands as $2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2$. Collecting like terms:

  • $\dot\theta_1^2$ coefficient:  $\tfrac{1}{2}m_1 L_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2\big[L_1^2 + L_2^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\big]$
  • $\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2$ coefficient:  $m_2\big[L_2^2 + L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\big]$
  • $\dot\theta_2^2$ coefficient:  $\tfrac{1}{2}m_2 L_2^2$
  • Potential energy:  $-(m_1+m_2)gL_1\sin\theta_1 - m_2 g L_2 \sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$

This grouped form is exactly what we need to compute the Euler–Lagrange partial derivatives cleanly.

§ 7

Euler–Lagrange for $\theta_1$ — torque $\tau_1$

Apply $\tau_1 = \dfrac{d}{dt}\!\left(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\theta_1}\right) - \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_1}$.

(a) Partial with respect to $\dot\theta_1$:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\theta_1} = m_1 L_1^2 \dot\theta_1 + m_2\big[(L_1^2 + L_2^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2)\dot\theta_1 + (L_2^2 + L_1L_2\cos\theta_2)\dot\theta_2\big]$$

(b) Time derivative. Note $\cos\theta_2$ depends on time through $\theta_2$, and $\tfrac{d}{dt}\cos\theta_2 = -\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2$:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\theta_1}\right) =\;& m_1 L_1^2 \ddot\theta_1 \\ &+ m_2\big[(L_1^2 + L_2^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2)\ddot\theta_1 + (L_2^2 + L_1L_2\cos\theta_2)\ddot\theta_2 \\ &\qquad - 2L_1L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2\dot\theta_1 - L_1L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2^2\big] \end{aligned}$$

(c) Partial with respect to $\theta_1$. The kinetic energy has no $\theta_1$ dependence (only $\theta_2$ appears, via $\cos\theta_2$), so only the potential contributes:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_1} = -(m_1+m_2)gL_1\cos\theta_1 - m_2 g L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$$

(d) Assemble $\tau_1$:

$$\begin{aligned} \tau_1 =\;& m_1 L_1^2 \ddot\theta_1 \\ &+ m_2\big[(L_1^2 + L_2^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2)\ddot\theta_1 + (L_2^2 + L_1L_2\cos\theta_2)\ddot\theta_2 \\ &\qquad - 2L_1L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2\dot\theta_1 - L_1L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2^2\big] \\ &+ (m_1+m_2)gL_1\cos\theta_1 + m_2 g L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{aligned}$$

Two velocity-dependent terms

The $-2L_1L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2\dot\theta_1$ term is a Coriolis term; $-L_1L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2^2$ is a centripetal term. Both come from the time derivative of $\cos\theta_2$ — they aren't optional.

§ 8

Euler–Lagrange for $\theta_2$ — torque $\tau_2$

Now $\tau_2 = \dfrac{d}{dt}\!\left(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\theta_2}\right) - \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_2}$.

(a) Partial with respect to $\dot\theta_2$ — pick out every term in $L$ that contains $\dot\theta_2$:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\theta_2} = m_2\big[(L_2^2 + L_1L_2\cos\theta_2)\dot\theta_1 + L_2^2\dot\theta_2\big]$$

(b) Time derivative:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\theta_2}\right) = m_2\big[(L_2^2 + L_1L_2\cos\theta_2)\ddot\theta_1 + L_2^2\ddot\theta_2 - L_1L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2\dot\theta_1\big]$$

(c) Partial with respect to $\theta_2$. From kinetic energy, $\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta_2}\cos\theta_2 = -\sin\theta_2$, contributing $-m_2 L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$. From potential energy, $-m_2 g L_2 \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$. Subtracting in the Euler–Lagrange equation flips both signs.

(d) Assemble $\tau_2$ (the kinetic-energy partial cancels a velocity term from part (b); we present the canonical compact form):

$$\begin{aligned} \tau_2 =\;& m_2\big[(L_2^2 + L_1L_2\cos\theta_2)\ddot\theta_1 + L_2^2\ddot\theta_2 \\ &\qquad + L_1L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1^2\big] \\ &+ m_2 g L_2 \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{aligned}$$

Sanity check

At $\theta_2 = 0$ (arm fully extended) the inertia matrix entries are maximal and the $\sin\theta_2$ coupling terms vanish — the arm momentarily looks like a single rigid pendulum. That's the limit you can use to check signs.

§ 9

What you now have, and where it goes next

You've taken a planar 2-link arm with tip masses and produced two coupled, nonlinear, second-order ODEs in $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ — the manipulator's equations of motion in the standard $M(\theta)\ddot\theta + C(\theta,\dot\theta)\dot\theta + g(\theta) = \tau$ form, just written out term-by-term rather than as matrices.

In Module 3 we'll:

  • Repackage $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ into the explicit mass matrix $M(\theta)$, Coriolis/centripetal matrix $C(\theta,\dot\theta)$, and gravity vector $g(\theta)$.
  • Use these to simulate the arm forward in time, and to design controllers (computed torque, PD+gravity-compensation).
  • Generalise to distributed-mass links via inertia tensors and to $n$-link manipulators.

If this derivation already feels mechanical to you, you're ready for the matrix-form rewrite. If it still feels like magic, redo it on paper before next class — that's the single highest-leverage hour you can spend.

Practice — try these on your own

Small activities to test your grip on today's ideas. Try each one first, then reveal the worked solution to compare. Not a hand-in — purely for your own self-check.

1 · Re-derive $v_2^2$ from scratch

Without looking at §4, start from $\dot{x}_2$ and $\dot{y}_2$ and derive

$$v_2^2 = L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2).$$

Write every line. The goal is to feel why the coupling term collapses to $\cos\theta_2$ — which identity does the work, and at which step?

Self-check after you've tried
  • Final expression must contain exactly three terms.
  • The coupling term must be $\cos\theta_2$ (the elbow angle), not $\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$ or $\cos\theta_1$.
  • At $\theta_2 = 0$ your $v_2^2$ should reduce to $(L_1+L_2)^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2\cdot\text{something})^2$ — check that limit makes geometric sense.
Show worked solution →

Starting from

$$\begin{aligned} \dot{x}_2 &= -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1 - L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \\ \dot{y}_2 &= L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2) \end{aligned}$$

Square each and add. Write $A = L_1\dot\theta_1$ and $B = L_2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$ to keep the algebra readable. Then

$$\dot{x}_2^2 = A^2\sin^2\theta_1 + B^2\sin^2(\theta_1+\theta_2) + 2AB\sin\theta_1\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$$ $$\dot{y}_2^2 = A^2\cos^2\theta_1 + B^2\cos^2(\theta_1+\theta_2) + 2AB\cos\theta_1\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$$

Add them. The $A^2$ terms combine via $\sin^2\theta_1+\cos^2\theta_1 = 1$ to give $A^2$. Likewise the $B^2$ terms give $B^2$. The two cross terms combine using

$$\sin\theta_1\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) + \cos\theta_1\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) = \cos\big((\theta_1+\theta_2)-\theta_1\big) = \cos\theta_2.$$

So

$$v_2^2 = A^2 + B^2 + 2AB\cos\theta_2.$$

Substituting back $A$ and $B$:

$$\boxed{\,v_2^2 = L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)\,}$$

The geometric meaning: only the relative angle $\theta_2$ between the links matters for the coupling, because $v_2^2$ is frame-invariant — rotating the whole arm by a fixed amount cannot change it.

2 · Sanity-check $\tau_1$ in the static, fully-extended pose

Plug $\dot\theta_1 = \dot\theta_2 = 0$, $\ddot\theta_1 = \ddot\theta_2 = 0$, and $\theta_2 = 0$ into the expression for $\tau_1$ from §7. What torque must the shoulder motor apply to hold the arm horizontally extended ($\theta_1 = 0$, $\theta_2 = 0$)? Does the answer match what you'd compute by elementary statics (moment of gravity about the shoulder)?

Self-check after you've tried
  • All inertial and velocity-dependent terms should vanish under the static assumption.
  • Your answer should depend on $m_1, m_2, L_1, L_2, g$ — and at $\theta_1 = 0$ it should be a pure gravity moment with both masses contributing.
  • The sign should be positive (the motor pushes up against gravity).
Show worked solution →

With every velocity and acceleration zero, $\tau_1$ collapses to just the gravity terms:

$$\tau_1\big|_{\text{static}} = (m_1+m_2)gL_1\cos\theta_1 + m_2 g L_2 \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2).$$

Now evaluate at $\theta_1 = 0$ and $\theta_2 = 0$, so both cosines equal 1:

$$\boxed{\,\tau_1\big|_{\theta_1=\theta_2=0} = (m_1+m_2)gL_1 + m_2 g L_2\,}$$

Elementary statics check. The shoulder must support the moment of each mass about the shoulder pivot. Mass $m_1$ sits at horizontal distance $L_1$ from the shoulder, contributing a gravity moment of $m_1 g L_1$. Mass $m_2$ sits at distance $L_1 + L_2$, contributing $m_2 g (L_1 + L_2) = m_2 g L_1 + m_2 g L_2$. Adding:

$$\tau_{\text{statics}} = m_1 g L_1 + m_2 g L_1 + m_2 g L_2 = (m_1+m_2)gL_1 + m_2 g L_2.$$

This matches exactly — good. Whenever a derivation this long produces a result that agrees with a one-line moment-balance check, you should believe both the derivation and the check.

3 · Identify the inertia matrix $M(\theta)$

Look at $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ in §§7–8. The coefficients multiplying $\ddot\theta_1$ and $\ddot\theta_2$ form a $2\times 2$ symmetric matrix $M(\theta)$ such that the acceleration-dependent part of the dynamics is $M(\theta)\,\ddot\theta$. Write $M(\theta)$ explicitly as a $2\times 2$ matrix. Confirm it is symmetric.

Self-check after you've tried
  • $M$ should be $2\times 2$, symmetric, and depend only on $\theta_2$ (not on $\theta_1$).
  • $M_{11}$ should contain $m_1 L_1^2$ plus a long $m_2$ bracket.
  • $M_{22}$ should be $m_2 L_2^2$ (a constant).
  • $M_{12} = M_{21}$ should both equal $m_2(L_2^2 + L_1 L_2 \cos\theta_2)$.
Show worked solution →

Read off coefficients of $\ddot\theta_1$ and $\ddot\theta_2$ from $\tau_1$ (§7) and $\tau_2$ (§8):

  • From $\tau_1$: coefficient of $\ddot\theta_1$ is $m_1 L_1^2 + m_2(L_1^2 + L_2^2 + 2L_1 L_2 \cos\theta_2)$; coefficient of $\ddot\theta_2$ is $m_2(L_2^2 + L_1 L_2 \cos\theta_2)$.
  • From $\tau_2$: coefficient of $\ddot\theta_1$ is $m_2(L_2^2 + L_1 L_2 \cos\theta_2)$; coefficient of $\ddot\theta_2$ is $m_2 L_2^2$.

Assemble into a matrix:

$$M(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} m_1 L_1^2 + m_2\big(L_1^2 + L_2^2 + 2L_1 L_2 \cos\theta_2\big) & m_2\big(L_2^2 + L_1 L_2 \cos\theta_2\big) \\ m_2\big(L_2^2 + L_1 L_2 \cos\theta_2\big) & m_2 L_2^2 \end{bmatrix}$$

The off-diagonal entries are equal, so $M$ is symmetric — exactly as the Lagrangian formulation guarantees for any manipulator with a kinetic energy quadratic in $\dot\theta$. Notice $M$ depends only on $\theta_2$ (the relative elbow angle); this is the same frame-invariance argument from homework problem 1 — the absolute orientation $\theta_1$ cannot change the kinetic energy.
