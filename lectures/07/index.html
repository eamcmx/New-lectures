What this lecture covers

We unpack each piece of the manipulator equation $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ using the 5-DOF Mitsubishi RV-M1 as a concrete case study.

For each term — the configuration-dependent inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$, the velocity-dependent vector $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$, and the gravity vector $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — we'll read it off the existing five_dof_dynamics.m implementation, interpret it physically, and run a small MATLAB experiment to feel its size. We close with a layered controller (PD → PD+gravity → full inverse dynamics) and a sketch of where the original code is begging to be optimised.

§ 1

The inertia matrix D(q): configuration-dependent mass

$\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is a $5 \times 5$ symmetric, positive-definite matrix whose entries change with the robot's pose. The diagonal entry $D_{ii}$ is the self-inertia seen at joint $i$ when only that joint accelerates; the off-diagonal $D_{ij}$ is the inertial coupling — the torque that appears at joint $i$ when joint $j$ alone accelerates.

For the RV-M1 the structural numbers tell the story. $D_{11}$ (base rotation) carries the full 5-link load and is biggest when the arm is stretched out horizontally. $D_{22}$ (shoulder) varies dramatically as the 250 mm upper arm sweeps. $D_{33}$ (elbow) sees mostly the 160 mm forearm and wrist. $D_{44}$ and $D_{55}$ are small wrist inertias.

The off-diagonal couplings are why "control one joint at a time" is a lie: $D_{23}$ is large because the forearm bridges shoulder and elbow, and $D_{13}$ grows as the arm extends and the moment arm lengthens.

Think of D(q) as the arm's mass matrix as a function of pose — same hardware, but it feels heavier to the motors in some configurations than in others.

Two invariants

$\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is always symmetric and positive-definite. The Lagrangian formulation $\sum_k \mathrm{tr}(U_{ki} J_k U_{kj}^\top)$ guarantees symmetry by construction; positive-definiteness guarantees the kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^\top\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ is strictly positive whenever the robot is moving.

Why off-diagonals matter for control

If $D_{ij} \neq 0$, accelerating joint $j$ pushes joint $i$. A decoupled PID per joint cannot see this — it just experiences a disturbance.

§ 2

Velocity-dependent forces h(q, q̇)

The vector $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ collects the centrifugal terms (proportional to $\dot q_i^2$) and the Coriolis terms (proportional to $\dot q_i \dot q_j$ with $i \neq j$). In the code these are the 125 Christoffel symbols h111, h112, … assembled into the h vector.

Physically they are "phantom" forces: zero when the robot is still, but quadratic in joint speeds when it moves. On the RV-M1 they appear as outward centrifugal pull on the 250 mm and 160 mm links during fast base rotation, and as coupling forces at the elbow whenever shoulder and base move together.

The quadratic scaling is the operational headline — doubling the joint speed roughly quadruples the velocity-dependent torque. That is why these terms are negligible at jog speeds and decisive at production speeds.

If you've ever felt your coffee slosh when you turn a corner too fast, you've felt h(q,q̇).

Speed thresholds on the RV-M1

Below ~0.5 rad/s, $\mathbf{h}$ is under 1% of total torque — a plain PID copes. Between 1–3 rad/s it climbs to 5–15%. Above 3 rad/s it crosses 15–40% and you need dynamic compensation. Above 8 rad/s it dominates and only the full model will track.

Isolating the Coriolis component

Run the model with only joint 1 spinning, then with joints 1 and 2 spinning at the same rate. The difference in torque is the pure interaction (Coriolis) term.

§ 3

Gravity term c(q): the static load

Gravity is the most predictable term and on the RV-M1 it usually dominates the static torque budget. In the code it is the cc vector. Joint 1 sees almost no gravity torque (it rotates about a vertical axis); joint 2 sees the most, because it must hold up everything beyond the shoulder; joint 3 sees a moderate load from the forearm and wrist; joints 4 and 5 see only the end-effector.

For a single revolute joint with a fixed mass beyond it, the gravity torque follows a $\sin\theta$ law: maximum when the arm is horizontal, zero when the link is vertical (pointing straight up or straight down). Because $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ depends only on pose — not on velocity or acceleration — it is the ideal candidate for feedforward compensation.

Gravity never sleeps. If your PD controller has to fight gravity with proportional gain alone, you'll always sag below the setpoint.

Extracting c(q) from the code

Calling five_dof_dynamics(q, zeros(5,1), zeros(5,1), 'rad') returns $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ on its own — zero velocity kills $\mathbf{h}$, zero acceleration kills $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$, what remains is gravity.

§ 4

Reading D(q) out of five_dof_dynamics

The shipped five_dof_dynamics returns the total torque $\boldsymbol{\tau}$, not the inertia matrix. But because $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$, we can recover $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ column-by-column. Set $\dot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{0}$ to kill the Coriolis term, then drive $\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{e}_j$ (a unit acceleration at joint $j$). What you get is $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{e}_j + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$, i.e. the $j$-th column of $\mathbf{D}$ plus gravity. Subtract $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — itself one extra call with zero velocity and acceleration — and you have $\mathbf{D}(:,j)$.

This trick lets us run forward dynamics in the simulation loop: given a commanded $\boldsymbol{\tau}$, solve $\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\tau} - \mathbf{h} - \mathbf{c})$ without rewriting the inverse-dynamics code.

Cost

Recovering $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ this way costs $n+1$ inverse-dynamics calls per step ($n=5$ unit accelerations + one gravity call). Fine for offline simulation, far from real-time-friendly for fast control loops.

Recover the 5x5 inertia matrix using only the existing inverse-dynamics functionmatlab
function D = get_inertia_matrix_fd(q)
    n = length(q);
    D = zeros(n,n);
    tau_0 = five_dof_dynamics(q, zeros(n,1), zeros(n,1), 'rad');  % gravity only
    for j = 1:n
        qdd = zeros(n,1); qdd(j) = 1;
        tau = five_dof_dynamics(q, zeros(n,1), qdd, 'rad');
        D(:,j) = tau - tau_0;
    end
end

§ 5

PD + gravity compensation, end to end

With $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ on tap, the simplest control law that actually works on a serial arm is PD with gravity feedforward:

$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = K_p(\mathbf{q}_d - \mathbf{q}) + K_d(\dot{\mathbf{q}}_d - \dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}).$$

The PD pair handles transient tracking; the gravity term removes the steady-state droop that proportional gain alone can never erase.

The full closed-loop simulation below combines this controller with the forward-dynamics trick from the previous section. We pick joint 2, command a 45° step, and watch position, velocity, acceleration and torque. The control gains for joint 2 — $K_p = 60$, $K_d = 10$ in the comments — are an empirically tuned compromise between rise time and overshoot.

This is the smallest controller you can build that respects the physics. Anything simpler will fight gravity; anything fancier is overkill until you start tracking trajectories.

Why gravity compensation matters

Comment out the + tau_g term and run the same step. The arm will settle below the setpoint with a constant offset proportional to the gravity load divided by $K_p$ — exactly the steady-state error PD alone cannot eliminate.

Tuning intuition

Low gains → slow, sluggish response. High gains → fast, but the inertia + velocity terms (still uncompensated here) start exciting oscillations. The $K_p=60$, $K_d=10$ pair on joint 2 is critically-damped-ish for the RV-M1 at this pose.

PD + gravity controller (reusable)matlab
function tau_total = simple_rvm1_controller(q_desired, q_current, qd_current, Kp, Kd)
    if nargin < 4
        Kp = diag([50, 100, 80, 30, 20]);
    end
    if nargin < 5
        Kd = diag([5, 10, 8, 3, 2]);
    end
    pos_error = q_desired - q_current;
    vel_error = -qd_current;            % desired velocities are zero

    tau_pd      = Kp * pos_error + Kd * vel_error;
    tau_gravity = five_dof_dynamics(q_current, zeros(5,1), zeros(5,1), 'rad');
    tau_total   = tau_pd + tau_gravity;
end
Closed-loop simulation: 45 deg step on joint 2matlab
clear; clc;
dt = 0.01; T = 5;
t = 0:dt:T;
n = 5;

q  = zeros(n,1);
qd = zeros(n,1);
q_desired = zeros(n,1);
q_desired(2) = deg2rad(45);

Kp = diag([0, 6, 0, 0, 0]);
Kd = diag([0, 1, 0, 0, 0]);

q_hist = zeros(n,length(t));
for k = 1:length(t)
    tau_pd = Kp*(q_desired - q) + Kd*(-qd);
    tau_g  = five_dof_dynamics(q, zeros(n,1), zeros(n,1), 'rad');
    tau    = tau_pd + tau_g;

    D   = get_inertia_matrix_fd(q);
    qdd = D \ (tau - tau_g);

    qd = qd + qdd*dt;
    q  = q  + qd*dt;
    q_hist(:,k) = q;
end

plot(t, rad2deg(q_hist(2,:)), 'b', 'LineWidth', 2); hold on;
plot(t, rad2deg(q_desired(2))*ones(size(t)), 'r--');
xlabel('t (s)'); ylabel('q_2 (deg)'); grid on;
§ 6

Slicing the torque budget by component

Because the dynamics function is linear in $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ and quadratic in $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ but additive across the three groups, we can isolate each component with a few clever calls. Gravity alone: zero $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$, zero $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$. Inertial alone: zero $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$, then subtract gravity. Velocity alone: zero $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$, then subtract gravity. The remainder is what each piece of physics is asking from each motor.

This is the most useful diagnostic in the module: before you tune a controller, run this on the worst-case operating point and see who is asking for the torque. If gravity dominates, feedforward $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$. If inertial terms dominate, you need a model-based controller. If velocity terms dominate, your trajectory is too fast for the hardware.

Reading the printout

Joint 1 usually shows ~100% inertial / velocity — gravity has no moment arm about the vertical axis. Joint 2 in a horizontal pose at low speed will show ~100% gravity. Crank the speed and watch the percentages flip.

Component breakdown for a given (q, qd, qdd)matlab
function analyze_dynamic_components(q, qd, qdd)
    tau_total    = five_dof_dynamics(q, qd, qdd, 'rad');
    tau_gravity  = five_dof_dynamics(q, zeros(5,1), zeros(5,1), 'rad');
    tau_inertial = five_dof_dynamics(q, zeros(5,1), qdd, 'rad') - tau_gravity;
    tau_velocity = five_dof_dynamics(q, qd, zeros(5,1), 'rad') - tau_gravity;

    fprintf('                J1     J2     J3     J4     J5\n');
    fprintf('Total:     %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f\n', tau_total);
    fprintf('Gravity:   %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f\n', tau_gravity);
    fprintf('Inertial:  %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f\n', tau_inertial);
    fprintf('Velocity:  %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f %6.2f\n', tau_velocity);
end

§ 7

A three-rung control ladder

Different operating regimes deserve different controllers, and all three can be built on top of the same five_dof_dynamics function.

Level 1 — Plain PD. Cheap, robust, but suffers steady-state droop under gravity and tracks poorly at speed. A reasonable baseline.

Level 2 — PD + gravity compensation. Removes the steady-state droop entirely. Best return-on-effort upgrade you will ever make. Adequate up to a few rad/s on the RV-M1.

Level 3 — Full inverse-dynamics (computed torque). Feedforward the whole RHS — inertia, Coriolis, gravity — using the desired trajectory $(\mathbf{q}_d, \dot{\mathbf{q}}_d, \ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d)$, and let a small PD term mop up tracking error. This is what you reach for when you need precision at speed.

Climb the ladder one rung at a time. Each rung costs you one more call to the dynamics function and buys you measurably better behaviour.

Computed torque, in one line

$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \underbrace{\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})}_{\text{feedforward}} + \underbrace{K_p(\mathbf{q}_d-\mathbf{q}) + K_d(\dot{\mathbf{q}}_d-\dot{\mathbf{q}})}_{\text{feedback}}.$ In code, the feedforward is exactly one call to five_dof_dynamics(q, qd_current, qdd_desired, 'rad').

Level 3 controller using only the inverse-dynamics functionmatlab
function tau_full = full_dynamic_control(q_desired, qd_desired, qdd_desired, q_current, qd_current)
    tau_feedforward = five_dof_dynamics(q_current, qd_current, qdd_desired, 'rad');

    Kp = diag([50, 100, 80, 30, 20]);
    Kd = diag([5, 10, 8, 3, 2]);

    pos_error = q_desired  - q_current;
    vel_error = qd_desired - qd_current;

    tau_feedback = Kp * pos_error + Kd * vel_error;
    tau_full     = tau_feedforward + tau_feedback;
end

§ 8

Where the legacy code is bleeding cycles

The shipped five_dof_dynamics.m is a faithful, line-by-line transcription of the Lagrangian formulation. That makes it pedagogically beautiful and computationally awful. Three things stand out.

Redundant matrix products. The code computes 125 Christoffel symbols h111, h112, … each as an explicit trace(U*J*U'). Many of the intermediate $U \cdot J$ products are reused dozens of times and recomputed every time. A single pass that caches the products would already give a large speedup.

Storage of every U matrix. All 15 first-order and 125 second-order $U$ matrices are kept around. A recursive Newton–Euler formulation avoids them entirely and is $O(n)$ instead of the Lagrangian's $O(n^4)$.

Hardcoded geometry. Link lengths and masses (L1 = 0.300; ... m1 = 5; ...) live inside the function. Parameterising them — or, better, code-generating the dynamics from a symbolic robot description — is the path to a reusable library.

The three modern moves

1) Use MATLAB's Symbolic Toolbox to derive and simplify the expressions once, then code-generate them. 2) Switch to recursive Newton–Euler for $O(n)$ real-time evaluation. 3) Parameterise on a robot struct so the same code works for 3-, 5-, 7-DOF arms.

Small activities to test your grip on today's ideas. Try each one first, then reveal the worked solution to compare. Nothing here is graded — this is for you.

1 · Extract one column of D(q) by hand

Without running MATLAB, use the manipulator equation $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ to derive how you would recover the third column $\mathbf{D}(:,3)$ from at most two calls to five_dof_dynamics. State the values of $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ and $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ for each call and the arithmetic that combines the two outputs.

Self-check after you've tried
  • Both calls should use $\dot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{0}$
  • The two $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ vectors should differ only in the third entry
  • The final answer should be a $5\times 1$ vector, not a scalar
Show worked solution

Set $\dot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{0}$ in both calls so that $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) = \mathbf{0}$ disappears.

Call A — gravity only. Use $\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{0}$. The manipulator equation collapses to

$$\boldsymbol{\tau}_A = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\cdot\mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}).$$

Call B — unit acceleration at joint 3. Use $\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{e}_3 = [0,0,1,0,0]^\top$. Then

$$\boldsymbol{\tau}_B = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{e}_3 + \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{D}(:,3) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}).$$

Combine. Subtract:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{D}(:,3) \;=\; \boldsymbol{\tau}_B - \boldsymbol{\tau}_A.}$$

This is exactly the trick used in get_inertia_matrix_fd — repeating it for $j = 1,\dots,5$ (with one shared call for $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$) reconstructs the full $5\times 5$ inertia matrix at the cost of $n+1 = 6$ inverse-dynamics evaluations.

2 · Predict the gravity torque at joint 2

Treat joint 2 of the RV-M1 in isolation: imagine the 250 mm upper arm with everything beyond it lumped into a point mass $M$ at the end of the link, and the link itself idealised as massless. With the shoulder angle $\theta_2$ measured from horizontal, write down the gravity torque $\tau_2^{\text{grav}}(\theta_2)$ in closed form. Then predict (i) the angle at which it is maximum, (ii) the angles at which it is zero, and (iii) what fraction of the maximum it is at $\theta_2 = 30°$.

Self-check after you've tried
  • Your formula should have a $\cos$ or $\sin$, not both
  • The answer at $\theta_2 = 0$ should equal the maximum if you measured from horizontal, or zero if you measured from vertical
  • The $30°$ fraction should come out to either $\cos 30° \approx 0.866$ or $\sin 30° = 0.5$
Show worked solution

With $\theta_2$ measured from horizontal, the moment arm of gravity about joint 2 is $L_2 \cos\theta_2$ where $L_2 = 0.250\text{ m}$. The gravity force on the lumped mass is $Mg$ acting downward, so the torque about joint 2 is

$$\tau_2^{\text{grav}}(\theta_2) \;=\; M g L_2 \cos\theta_2.$$

(i) Maximum. $\cos\theta_2$ is maximised at $\theta_2 = 0$ (arm horizontal). $\tau_{2,\max}^{\text{grav}} = M g L_2.$

(ii) Zeros. $\cos\theta_2 = 0$ at $\theta_2 = \pm 90°$ (arm pointing straight up or straight down). Both make physical sense — a vertical link has zero gravitational moment about its hinge.

(iii) Fraction at $30°$.

$$\frac{\tau_2^{\text{grav}}(30°)}{\tau_{2,\max}^{\text{grav}}} \;=\; \cos 30° \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \;\approx\; 0.866.$$

So at $30°$ above horizontal, joint 2 is still carrying roughly 87% of its worst-case gravity torque. (If you had instead measured $\theta_2$ from the vertical, you'd get $\sin\theta_2$ and the maximum would sit at $\theta_2 = 90°$; the physics is identical, only the angle convention changes.) The plotted curve in section 3 is exactly this shape, with the small wrinkles coming from the fact that the real RV-M1 has distributed mass along the link, not a single lumped mass at the tip.

3 · Quadratic scaling of centrifugal torque

From the MATLAB experiment in section 2, the joint-1 torque magnitude at $\dot q_1 = 1\text{ rad/s}$ is some baseline value $\tau_0$. Predict — from first principles, without re-running the code — what the torque magnitude should be at $\dot q_1 = 2, 5,$ and $10\text{ rad/s}$ when all other joint speeds are zero and the pose is held fixed. Then explain in one sentence why the scaling is what it is.

Self-check after you've tried
  • Your predictions should be multiples of $\tau_0$, not absolute numbers
  • The ratio between the $5\text{ rad/s}$ and $1\text{ rad/s}$ entries should be 25, not 5
  • The one-sentence explanation should mention that $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ is quadratic in $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$
Show worked solution

The velocity-dependent vector $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ is built from Christoffel symbols of the form $h_{ijk}\,\dot q_j \dot q_k$. With only joint 1 spinning, every term in $\mathbf{h}$ that survives is proportional to $\dot q_1^2$ (the pure centrifugal contribution); all cross-terms $\dot q_1 \dot q_j$ for $j\neq 1$ vanish because $\dot q_j = 0$. Hence

$$\|\boldsymbol{\tau}(\dot q_1)\| \;\propto\; \dot q_1^{\,2}.$$

Using $\tau_0 = \|\boldsymbol{\tau}(1)\|$ as the baseline:

$\dot q_1$ (rad/s)Scaling factorPredicted $\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|$
1$1^2 = 1$$\tau_0$
2$2^2 = 4$$4\tau_0$
5$5^2 = 25$$25\tau_0$
10$10^2 = 100$$100\tau_0$

One-line explanation. Centrifugal torque is quadratic in joint velocity because $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ contains products $\dot q_j\dot q_k$, so doubling the speed quadruples the term — which is precisely why these forces can be ignored at jog speeds and absolutely cannot be ignored at production speeds.

(Small caveat: gravity $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ is also baked into what five_dof_dynamics returns, so on the bench you'd want to subtract a zero-velocity baseline first to isolate the centrifugal part. With that subtraction, the $1 : 4 : 25 : 100$ ratio is exact.)

4 · Why PD without gravity compensation droops

Consider joint 2 alone with proportional gain $K_p$ holding it at a setpoint $q_d$ where the gravity torque is $\tau_g$. Assume the joint has come to rest ($\dot q_2 = \ddot q_2 = 0$) and ignore friction. Derive the steady-state error $e_{\text{ss}} = q_d - q_2$ as a closed-form expression in $\tau_g$ and $K_p$. Then plug in $\tau_g = 15\text{ N·m}$ and the lesson's value $K_p = 60\text{ N·m/rad}$ and convert the answer to degrees.

Self-check after you've tried
  • Your formula should not contain $K_d$ (the derivative gain plays no role at rest)
  • The numerical answer should be a few degrees, not microradians or radians
  • The steady-state error should shrink as $K_p$ grows, not grow
Show worked solution

Equation of motion at rest. With $\dot q_2 = 0$ and $\ddot q_2 = 0$, the joint equation reduces to a torque balance:

$$\underbrace{K_p\,(q_d - q_2) + K_d\,(0 - 0)}_{\text{PD output}} \;=\; \underbrace{\tau_g}_{\text{gravity load}}.$$

The derivative term vanishes identically because velocities are zero on both sides.

Solve for the steady-state error.

$$K_p\, e_{\text{ss}} \;=\; \tau_g \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{e_{\text{ss}} \;=\; \frac{\tau_g}{K_p}.}$$

Numerical example. With $\tau_g = 15\text{ N·m}$ and $K_p = 60\text{ N·m/rad}$:

$$e_{\text{ss}} \;=\; \frac{15}{60} \;=\; 0.25\text{ rad} \;\approx\; 14.3°.$$

Interpretation. Plain PD will sit 14° below the setpoint indefinitely — the proportional spring is just strong enough to balance gravity at that offset and no stronger. Cranking $K_p$ shrinks the droop ($K_p \to \infty \Rightarrow e_{\text{ss}} \to 0$), but high gains also amplify sensor noise and excite unmodelled dynamics. Adding the gravity feedforward $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ cancels $\tau_g$ on the RHS exactly, so the PD term only has to handle transient tracking and $e_{\text{ss}} = 0$ without any heroic gain tuning. That's the entire reason every chapter in this module insists on + tau_g.
