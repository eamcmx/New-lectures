Composite rotations & animating the Karovka

Six events, one matrix — pre- vs. post-multiplying our way around fixed and mobile axes.

Module Robot Motion & Rotations Course Kinematics of Manipulators Builds on Lecture 04 Tools MATLAB · pen & paper
Last class we drew the little Karovka — eight points, twelve edges, one identity matrix sitting at the origin. Today we actually move it. Six rotations in a row, some about the fixed frame $XYZ$, some about the mobile frame $UVW$, and we'll see why where you place a matrix in the product is the whole game. Then we'll wrap it into a function, animate it with six for-loops, and finish with a puzzle: how many different event sequences land on the same composite matrix?
§ 00

What this lecture covers

We pick up the Karovka from last lecture and put it through a six-event sequence of rotations, mixing fixed-axis ($X$, $Y$, $Z$) and mobile-axis ($U$, $V$, $W$) motions. The goals:

  • write the composite rotation matrix by hand from a sequence of events,
  • code the animation as six chained for-loops in MATLAB,
  • refactor that into a reusable compMatrix(t1..t6) function,
  • and finally reverse the question — given a composite matrix, find different event sequences that produce the same final pose.
If you can write the matrix on paper and predict where the box ends up — without running MATLAB — you've got the intuition we're building.

Axis aliases

Mobile-frame $U \equiv X$, $V \equiv Y$, $W \equiv Z$ — the rotation matrix is the same, but where you place it in the product is not.

§ 01

Recap — the composite rotation rule

Every motion starts from the identity matrix $I$. Each new event either pre-multiplies $I$ (when the rotation is taken about the fixed reference frame) or post-multiplies it (when the rotation is about the mobile frame attached to the box).

So a sequence of events grows the product outward from the identity in both directions: fixed-frame motions stack on the left, mobile-frame motions stack on the right. The order within each side preserves the temporal order of the events.

$$R_C \;=\; \underbrace{R_{f_n}\cdots R_{f_2}\,R_{f_1}}_{\text{fixed-frame, left of }I}\,\cdot\,I\,\cdot\,\underbrace{R_{m_1}\,R_{m_2}\cdots R_{m_k}}_{\text{mobile-frame, right of }I}$$
Think of $I$ as the anchor — the world before anything moved. Everything you do to the world stacks to its left; everything you do to the box stacks to its right.
The mantra

Fixed → pre-multiply. Mobile → post-multiply. Identity stays in the middle until the bookkeeping is done.

general shape of the compositetxt
R_C  =  ... · R_fixed_2 · R_fixed_1 · I · R_mobile_1 · R_mobile_2 · ...
§ 02

Today's sequence — six events

We define a concrete sequence of six rotations applied to the Karovka, in order. Each event names its axis (fixed or mobile) and its angle.

  1. Rotation about $X$ (fixed) — $40°$
  2. Rotation about $Z$ (fixed) — $170°$
  3. Rotation about $W$ (mobile) — $200°$
  4. Rotation about $Y$ (fixed) — $70°$
  5. Rotation about $V$ (mobile) — $100°$
  6. Rotation about $W$ (mobile) — $120°$

Three of these ($X$, $Z$, $Y$) act on the fixed frame, three ($W$, $V$, $W$) on the mobile frame. That mix is what makes the bookkeeping interesting.

Pick angles you can eyeball in the animation later — round numbers help you sanity-check the final pose.
Watch the bookkeeping

Six events, six $\theta_i$. Number them by the time they happen, not by their position in the final matrix.

§ 03

Building the composite matrix by hand

Starting from $I$ and applying the rule, the six events arrange themselves around the identity like this:

$$R_C \;=\; R_Y(\theta_4)\,R_Z(\theta_2)\,R_X(\theta_1)\;\cdot\;I\;\cdot\;R_Z(\theta_3)\,R_Y(\theta_5)\,R_Z(\theta_6)$$

Notice the substitutions: $R_W = R_Z$, $R_V = R_Y$, $R_U = R_X$. The mobile axes use the same rotation matrices as their fixed counterparts — what changes is which side of $I$ they sit on.

Homework: multiply this out by hand to get a single $3 \times 3$ matrix, and verify it matches MATLAB's numerical result at $\theta_1=40,\ \theta_2=170,\ \theta_3=200,\ \theta_4=70,\ \theta_5=100,\ \theta_6=120$.

Once you've done the multiplication once on paper, you'll never be confused about pre- vs. post- again.
Why the substitution works

A rotation about a mobile axis $W$ aligned (initially) with $Z$ uses the same numerical matrix $R_Z$. The mobile-ness is encoded by post-multiplication, not by the matrix itself.

Common slip

Mixing up $\theta_i$ ↔ matrix position. The leftmost matrix is the most recent fixed-frame event, not the first one.

composite matrix laid outmatlab
% Six events → one composite
% fixed (left of I): X(t1), Z(t2), Y(t4)
% mobile (right of I): W(t3)=Z, V(t5)=Y, W(t6)=Z

RC = rotaY(t4) * rotaZ(t2) * rotaX(t1) ...
     * eye(3) ...
     * rotaZ(t3) * rotaY(t5) * rotaZ(t6);

§ 04

Animating with six chained for-loops

To see the box move through each event, each rotation gets its own for-loop sweeping its angle from $0°$ to its target value in small steps (e.g. $0.5°$). Inside each loop we (a) build the running composite $R_C$, (b) apply it to the eight vertices of the Karovka, (c) clear the figure and redraw via the karovka(...) helper from last lecture.

The key gotcha: at every step, the previous events must already be baked into $R_C$. If you only write RC1, RC2, ... in isolation, every loop restarts the box from the origin and you get spinning-from-zero instead of a chained motion.

If your animation looks like the box keeps teleporting home between events — you forgot to chain the matrices.
Animation pitfall

Each for-loop must multiply onto the result of the previous event, not onto $I$. Otherwise you're animating six independent motions, not one composite trajectory.

chained six-loop structure (sketch)matlab
% Event 1 — fixed X
for t1 = 0:0.5:40
    RC1 = rotaX(t1);
    % apply RC1 to nodes, redraw karovka
end

% Event 2 — fixed Z, pre-multiplies previous
for t2 = 0:0.5:170
    RC2 = rotaZ(t2) * RC1;
    % apply, redraw
end

% Event 3 — mobile W, post-multiplies previous
for t3 = 0:0.5:200
    RC3 = RC2 * rotaZ(t3);
end

% Event 4 — fixed Y, pre-multiplies
for t4 = 0:0.5:70
    RC4 = rotaY(t4) * RC3;
end

% Event 5 — mobile V, post-multiplies
for t5 = 0:0.5:100
    RC5 = RC4 * rotaY(t5);
end

% Event 6 — mobile W, post-multiplies
for t6 = 0:0.5:120
    RC6 = RC5 * rotaZ(t6);
end

§ 05

Pre-multiply vs. post-multiply — the rule in code

Translating the matrix rule directly into MATLAB:

  • Fixed-axis event at step $k$: RC_k = R_axis(t_k) * RC_{k-1};
  • Mobile-axis event at step $k$: RC_k = RC_{k-1} * R_axis(t_k);

For our sequence ($X$-fixed, $Z$-fixed, $W$-mobile, $Y$-fixed, $V$-mobile, $W$-mobile) that's: pre, pre, post, pre, post, post. If you check the animation event by event, the box should rotate around the world axes for events 1, 2, 4 and around its own body axes for events 3, 5, 6.

Read the sequence "pre, pre, post, pre, post, post" out loud before you run the script — it's your sanity check.
How to tell from the animation

During a fixed-frame event, the world axes stay put and the box sweeps around them. During a mobile-frame event, the box's own axes stay put and the world appears to swing past it.

§ 06

Refactor — wrap it in a compMatrix function

Once the order is settled, the composite is just a deterministic product of six rotation matrices given six angles. So we lift it out of the animation loop into a function that takes $\theta_1\ldots\theta_6$ and returns the $3 \times 3$ composite $R_C$.

Inside the animation, every for-loop now just calls compMatrix(t1,t2,t3,t4,t5,t6) with the current value of its own $\theta$ and the persistent values for the others — the function bakes in which side of $I$ each angle belongs to.

The function is the matrix on paper, written once. The for-loops are the camera filming it.
Why this scales

Adding more events later means editing one function, not chasing pre-/post-multiplications through six separate loops.

Initialization

Initialize t1 = t2 = ... = t6 = 0; before the loops so the function is callable from frame one with a well-defined pose (the identity).

the composite as a functionmatlab
function RC = compMatrix(t1, t2, t3, t4, t5, t6)
    % E is the identity — kept explicit for teaching clarity
    E = eye(3);

    % Fixed-frame events (left of E), in order of multiplication:
    %   most recent first → Y(t4), Z(t2), X(t1)
    % Mobile-frame events (right of E), in order:
    %   W(t3)=Z, V(t5)=Y, W(t6)=Z

    RC = rotaY(t4) * rotaZ(t2) * rotaX(t1) ...
         * E ...
         * rotaZ(t3) * rotaY(t5) * rotaZ(t6);
end
calling it from the animationmatlab
t1=0; t2=0; t3=0; t4=0; t5=0; t6=0;

for t1 = 0:-0.5:-70   % negative sweep: note the -0.5 step
    RC = compMatrix(t1,t2,t3,t4,t5,t6);
    % apply RC to nodes, redraw karovka
end
% ...same pattern for t2 through t6

§ 07

Sweep direction matters — going negative

For a quick experiment we flipped event 1's target from $+40°$ to $-70°$. The for-loop syntax has to match: for t1 = 0:-0.5:-70. If you leave the step positive (0:0.5:-70) MATLAB silently produces an empty range and the loop never runs — the box looks frozen on that event.

This is a tiny syntactic point but a common cause of "why isn't anything happening?" during the animation.

MATLAB doesn't warn you about an empty range — it just skips the loop. Always check the sign of the step.
Empty-range trap

0 : 0.5 : -70 is an empty vector. Use 0 : -0.5 : -70 for a decreasing sweep.

§ 08

Sliding $I$ — same matrix, different sequence

Here's the deep idea. The product

$$R_Y(\theta_4)\,R_Z(\theta_2)\,R_X(\theta_1)\;I\;R_Z(\theta_3)\,R_Y(\theta_5)\,R_Z(\theta_6)$$

is a single $3 \times 3$ matrix. Where we write $I$ inside the product is a teaching device — it labels which factors are fixed-frame and which are mobile-frame.

If we slide $I$ to a different position, the numerical product is unchanged (because $A \cdot I \cdot B = A \cdot B$ for any matrices), but the interpretation changes: factors on $I$'s new left become fixed-frame events and factors on its new right become mobile-frame events. Same composite matrix, different sequence of events.

This is why a given end-pose can be reached by many different event sequences — and why "find a sequence that lands here" is not a unique problem.

Where you draw $I$ is a story you're telling about the motion. Move $I$, change the story — but never the destination.
Example reinterpretation

Put $I$ all the way to the left: every event becomes mobile-frame. Put it all the way to the right: every event becomes fixed-frame. Both yield the same final $R_C$.

Why this matters for design

When you're planning a manipulator trajectory you get to choose the interpretation that's easiest to think about — usually the one where the smallest number of events live on the mobile side.

three readings of the same matrixtxt
Same product R_C, three different event stories:

(A) I in the middle:
    fixed:  X, Z, Y          mobile: W, V, W

(B) I one slot right:
    fixed:  X, Z, Y, W       mobile: V, W

(C) I at the far right:
    fixed:  X, Z, Y, W, V, W mobile: (none)

§ 09

In-class practice

Define your own sequence of at least five events — pick axes (fixed or mobile) and angles freely. Compute the composite matrix $R_C$ for that sequence using compMatrix. Then construct two additional sequences that yield the same $R_C$ by sliding the position of $I$ in the product, and animate each one in MATLAB. The end poses should all coincide — if they don't, your pre-/post- bookkeeping is off somewhere.

Three sequences, one destination. If the third animation lands on top of the first, your matrix algebra is correct.
Sanity check

Print $R_C$ to four decimal places at the end of each animation. Identical floats → identical poses.
