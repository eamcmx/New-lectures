What this lecture covers

We pick up where Lecture 01 left off. You already have rotaX(t) and you can draw a static mobile frame on top of the fixed frame. From here we will:

  1. turn the per-axis plot code into a reusable drawSystem(R) function,
  2. wrap it in a for loop with hold on + clf + drawnow so the rotation actually animates,
  3. derive and code rotaY and rotaZ by the same tic-tac-toe / projection trick,
  4. and use a rotation matrix to map a point $A_{uvw}$ given in the mobile frame into its fixed-frame coordinates $A_{xyz}$.

By the end you should be able to spin the mobile frame around any single axis on screen and report the fixed-frame coordinates of a point that's rigidly attached to it.

If you can see the frame move and you can locate a point in it, you have the seed of forward kinematics.

§ 1

Recap — why two frames, and what $R_x$ looks like

Two frames live happily on top of each other until one of them moves. The fixed frame uses $x, y, z$; the mobile frame uses $u, v, w$. When the frames overlap perfectly, the rotation matrix is the identity — each mobile axis projects fully onto its matching fixed axis, so the diagonal is all ones:

x · u y · v z · w
$$ I_3 \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Rotate the mobile frame by $\theta$ around the $X$ axis and $u$ still lies on $x$, but $v$ and $w$ pick up cosine/sine projections onto $y$ and $z$. That gave us last lecture's $R_x$:

$$ R_x(\theta) \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} $$

A 1 in the matrix means "this mobile axis is still glued to that fixed axis" — and it forces zeros across its row and column.

Reading the matrix
Rows are the mobile axes ($u, v, w$, top to bottom); columns are the fixed axes ($x, y, z$, left to right). Each entry is the projection of one mobile axis onto one fixed axis.
rotaX.m — rotation about XMATLAB
function Rx = rotaX(t)
    Rx = [ 1,        0,         0;
           0,  cosd(t),  -sind(t);
           0,  sind(t),   cosd(t) ];
end

cosd and sind take their argument in degrees. Stay in degrees throughout this module — mixing with cos/sin (which expect radians) silently breaks every result you'll plot.

§ 2

Turning the plot code into a reusable function

Last time we plotted the rotated frame by hand: extract ux, uy, uz, then vx, vy, vz, then wx, wy, wz from the rotation matrix and draw three line segments from the origin. That's fine as a script — but to animate we'll need to call it many times. So we wrap it as a function that takes any 3×3 matrix and draws those three axes.

We also silence MATLAB's echoes with semicolons and pin the camera with view(30, 30) so every frame is drawn from the same angle.

A function is just a script with a name and a contract — give me a 3×3, I'll draw you a frame.

Identity = fixed frame
eye(3) builds the 3×3 identity (the letter spelling of "I", with a regular parenthesis and the integer 3 inside). Passing it to drawSystem draws the fixed frame — handy as a reference whenever you draw the mobile one.
drawSystem.m — draws any frame from its rotation matrixMATLAB
function drawSystem(R)
    ux = R(1,1); uy = R(2,1); uz = R(3,1);
    vx = R(1,2); vy = R(2,2); vz = R(3,2);
    wx = R(1,3); wy = R(2,3); wz = R(3,3);

    line([0 ux],[0 uy],[0 uz]);
    line([0 vx],[0 vy],[0 vz]);
    line([0 wx],[0 wy],[0 wz]);

    view(30,30);
end

Notice what each column of $R$ is doing: it's one mobile axis written in fixed-frame coordinates. The function reads the three columns and draws one line segment per axis from the origin. If you ever pass the transpose by mistake, you'll draw the inverse rotation — same axes, swapped roles.

Draw the fixed frame plus a rotated oneMATLAB
I = eye(3);
drawSystem(I);          % fixed frame
hold on
drawSystem(rotaX(30));  % mobile frame at 30°
§ 3

Animating with for, hold on, drawnow and clf

To see the rotation rather than the end state, sweep $\theta$ through a for loop and redraw at every step. Three MATLAB ingredients matter:

  • hold on — keep the fixed frame on screen while we redraw the mobile one.
  • drawnow — force MATLAB to flush the figure each iteration, not just at the end of the script.
  • clf — clear the figure between iterations so we don't leave a footprint of every intermediate pose.

The loop syntax for t = 0:0.1:60 advances $t$ from 0 to 60 in steps of 0.1. Without the explicit step, MATLAB walks by 1.

Without clf you get a flip-book of every angle layered on top of each other — pretty, but not an animation.

Why clf and not just hold off
hold off would only release the hold; clf actually wipes the figure so the next iteration starts from a blank canvas. Without it you get a fan of axes from every angle the loop visited.
MATLAB for syntax
Unlike many languages, you do not increment the counter manually. for t = start:step:stop does it for you; close the block with end. Trying to write t = t + 0.1 inside the loop body either crashes or skips values — manual incrementing belongs to while.
exercise1.m — animate rotation around X from 0° to 60°MATLAB
close all
I = eye(3);

for t = 0:0.1:60
    clf
    hold on
    Rx = rotaX(t);
    drawSystem(I);
    drawSystem(Rx);
    drawnow
end

With step 0.1 from $0$ to $60$ that's 601 redraws — on a slow machine you'll feel it. Drop drawnow and the loop completes in silence, painting only the final pose. Drop clf and you see the layered footprint instead of motion. Each ingredient is doing distinct work.

§ 4

Deriving $R_y$ by projection

Rotate the mobile frame by $\theta$ around the $Y$ axis. Now $v$ stays on $y$, so the matrix has a 1 in the middle position and zeros across that row and column. Read each remaining entry off the diagram by projecting the rotated $u$ and $w$ onto $x$ and $z$:

projection
leg
entry
u on x
adjacent
$+\cos\theta$
u on z
opposite
$+\sin\theta$
w on x
opposite (negative side)
$-\sin\theta$
w on z
adjacent
$+\cos\theta$

Assembling the table back into the 3×3:

$$ R_y(\theta) \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} $$

The 1 anchors the row and column it sits in to zeros — then it's just adjacent/opposite around the 2×2 block that's left.

Sign of sin
For $R_y$ the minus sign lives at the $(3,1)$ entry — the projection that points against the positive fixed axis under a right-hand-rule rotation. It is not at $(1,3)$. If you ever feel unsure, draw the rotated $w$ vector and ask: which fixed axis is it pointing away from?
rotaY.mMATLAB
function Ry = rotaY(t)
    Ry = [  cosd(t),  0,  sind(t);
            0,        1,  0;
           -sind(t),  0,  cosd(t) ];
end
exercise2.m — animate around YMATLAB
close all
I = eye(3);

for t = 0:0.1:60
    clf
    hold on
    Ry = rotaY(t);
    drawSystem(I);
    drawSystem(Ry);
    drawnow
end

Run it: $v$ stays glued to $y$ while $u$ and $w$ swing around in the $x$–$z$ plane. Same code structure as exercise 1 — only the matrix-building function changed.

§ 5

Deriving $R_z$ the same way

Rotation about $Z$: now $w$ stays on $z$, so the 1 sits in the bottom-right corner and zeros fill that row and column. The remaining 2×2 block lives in the top-left and holds the cosine/sine pattern, with the minus sign on $v$'s projection onto $x$ — the $(1,2)$ entry.

$$ R_z(\theta) \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Same trick, different corner — the 1 always sits on the axis you're rotating around.

Three matrices, one rule. The diagonal "1" picks the axis you're not changing; the 2×2 cos/sin block lives in the plane perpendicular to that axis; the right-hand rule decides where the minus lands.

rotaZ.mMATLAB
function Rz = rotaZ(t)
    Rz = [ cosd(t),  -sind(t),  0;
           sind(t),   cosd(t),  0;
           0,         0,        1 ];
end
exercise3.m — animate around ZMATLAB
close all
I = eye(3);

for t = 0:0.1:60
    clf
    hold on
    Rz = rotaZ(t);
    drawSystem(I);
    drawSystem(Rz);
    drawnow
end

Now $w$ holds still and the action is in the $x$–$y$ plane: $u$ and $v$ sweep around together. If you run exercises 1, 2 and 3 back-to-back you have the full single-axis family on screen — the same loop scaffolding three times.

§ 6

Transforming a point from the mobile frame to the fixed frame

Imagine a point $A$ rigidly attached to the mobile body. We measure it in mobile coordinates as

$$ A_{uvw} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 0.3 \\ 0.4 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix}. $$

When the two frames overlap, those numbers are also its $(x, y, z)$ coordinates. The moment the mobile frame rotates, the numbers in the fixed frame change — even though the point hasn't moved relative to the mobile body. $A_{uvw}$ stays put; $A_{xyz}$ updates.

The relationship is one matrix-vector product:

$$ A_{xyz} \;=\; R(\theta) \cdot A_{uvw} $$

A 3×3 times a 3×1 gives a 3×1 — the new fixed-frame coordinates. Each fixed coordinate is a dot product of $A_{uvw}$ with one row of $R$, which is just "how much of $A$ lands along this fixed axis once the mobile frame has been tilted". The same formula works for $R_x$, $R_y$ or $R_z$ — pick whichever single-axis rotation you're doing.

The rotation matrix isn't just a picture — it's the calculator that tells you where every point in the mobile body lives in the world.

The big idea
This is the seed of forward kinematics. Stack rotations (and later, translations) like this for every joint of an arm and you can locate the end-effector in the base frame. Everything we build over the next few lectures hangs off this one product.

Worked example — rotate the mobile frame by $\theta = -30°$ about $X$ and ask where $A$ lands in the fixed frame. With $\cos(-30°) \approx 0.8660$ and $\sin(-30°) = -0.5$, only the $y$ and $z$ components change (the rotation is about $X$, so the $u$-component is untouched):

$$ A_{xyz} \;=\; R_x(-30°) \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0.3 \\ 0.4 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix} \;\approx\; \begin{bmatrix} 0.3000 \\ 0.5964 \\ 0.2330 \end{bmatrix}. $$
Transform a point under a −30° rotation about XMATLAB
A_uvw = [0.3; 0.4; 0.5];
Rx    = rotaX(-30);
A_xyz = Rx * A_uvw

Drop the semicolon on the last line and MATLAB echoes the result to the console; keep the semicolon and the value lives in A_xyz for whatever you do next (printing it on the figure, drawing the point in the animation, etc.).
