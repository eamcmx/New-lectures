Lagrangian Formulation for Robot Dynamics

From link velocities to the full equation of motion $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})+\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})=\boldsymbol{\tau}$.

Kinematics told us where the end-effector is. Today we ask the harder question: what torques does each motor need to push it there? We will build the dynamic model piece by piece — first the link velocities, then the kinetic and potential energies, and finally the inertia, Coriolis, and gravity terms that snap together into a single elegant matrix equation. By the end of the hour you will recognise every symbol in $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{c}=\boldsymbol{\tau}$ — and you will have derived it yourself for a 2-DOF arm.

0Overview

What this lecture covers

This lecture develops the Lagrangian formulation of robot manipulator dynamics from first principles. We start by extending homogeneous transformation matrices from positions to velocities, then assemble the kinetic and potential energies of an $n$-link arm. From those energies we read off the three terms of the equation of motion: the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$, the Coriolis/centrifugal vector $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$, and the gravity vector $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$.

The payoff is the compact equation $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})+\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})=\boldsymbol{\tau}$, which we apply end-to-end to a 2-DOF planar arm and then discuss in terms of computational cost for real-time control.

If you remember nothing else, remember those three letters: $\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{h}$, $\mathbf{c}$. Inertia, motion-coupling, gravity. Every robot controller on the planet is doing battle with these three terms.

Learning objectives

Velocity propagation via transformation matrices · derivation of $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ from kinetic energy · Christoffel symbols and $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ · gravity terms from potential energy · full 2-DOF worked example · $O(n^4)$ vs recursive complexity.

Why Lagrangian, not Newton-Euler?

Lagrangian gives you a symbolic model with clear physical interpretation. Newton-Euler is faster computationally — but harder to reason about when designing controllers.

1§ 3.1

Velocity propagation through the kinematic chain

Robot kinematics already tells us how to express a point fixed in link $i$ in base coordinates using a product of homogeneous transforms:

$${}^0\mathbf{r}_i = {}^0\mathbf{A}_1\,{}^1\mathbf{A}_2\cdots{}^{i-1}\mathbf{A}_i\,{}^i\mathbf{r}_i.$$

Differentiating in time and applying the chain rule — noting that ${}^i\mathbf{r}_i$ is constant in frame $i$ — gives ${}^0\mathbf{v}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{i} \frac{\partial {}^0\mathbf{A}_i}{\partial q_j}\dot{q}_j\,{}^i\mathbf{r}_i$.

The partial derivative of a transformation matrix with respect to a joint variable has a beautiful closed form: $\frac{\partial {}^0\mathbf{A}_i}{\partial q_j} = {}^0\mathbf{A}_j\,\mathbf{Q}_j\,{}^j\mathbf{A}_i$, where $\mathbf{Q}_j$ is a fixed $4\times 4$ structural matrix that depends only on whether joint $j$ is revolute or prismatic. We bundle this into the velocity influence coefficient $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$.

The miracle of $\mathbf{Q}_j$: an entire family of partial derivatives collapses into one matrix multiplication with a constant sparse pattern.

Causality rule

$\mathbf{U}_{ij} = \mathbf{0}$ for $j > i$. Joints further out cannot move points on earlier links — only joints between the base and link $i$ contribute to its velocity.

2§ 3.2

Kinetic energy and the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$

Summing the kinetic energy of every link and rearranging the triple sum gives the total kinetic energy as a quadratic form in the joint velocities:

$$T = \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}}, \quad D_{jk}(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\mathbf{U}_{ij}\,\mathbf{J}_i\,\mathbf{U}_{ik}^{T}\right).$$

Here $\mathbf{J}_i$ is the pseudo-inertia matrix of link $i$ in its own frame, and the trace operation handles the fact that we are working in $4\times 4$ homogeneous coordinates. The matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is symmetric, positive definite, and configuration-dependent — three properties you will lean on constantly in control design.

The diagonal entry $D_{jj}$ is the effective inertia your $j$-th motor 'feels' when only joint $j$ is moving. The off-diagonal $D_{jk}$ is the inertial cross-talk between joints $j$ and $k$.

Three properties that matter

Symmetric ($D_{jk}=D_{kj}$) · positive definite (kinetic energy is always positive) · bounded for any finite configuration. Many control proofs hinge on these.

Algorithm 3.1 — inertia matrix in pseudocode
D ← zeros(n, n)
for i = 1..n:
    compute ^0A_1, ^0A_2, ..., ^0A_i
    for j = 1..i:
        for k = 1..i:
            D[j,k] += trace( U_ij · J_i · U_ik^T )
enforce symmetry: D[k,j] = D[j,k]

3§ 3.3

Coriolis and centrifugal terms via Christoffel symbols

Because $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ depends on configuration, the Euler-Lagrange equation $\tfrac{d}{dt}\!\left(\partial T/\partial\dot{q}_i\right) - \partial T/\partial q_i = \tau_i$ produces velocity-dependent forces in addition to the obvious $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ term. These collect into the Christoffel symbols:

$$\Gamma_{ijk} = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\tfrac{\partial D_{ij}}{\partial q_k} + \tfrac{\partial D_{ik}}{\partial q_j} - \tfrac{\partial D_{jk}}{\partial q_i}\right),\quad h_i = \sum_{j,k}\Gamma_{ijk}\,\dot{q}_j\dot{q}_k.$$

Terms with $j\ne k$ are Coriolis (two different joints moving create force on a third); terms with $j=k$ are centrifugal (a single joint's motion reacts on itself through the changing inertia distribution). A subtle but important property: $\dot{\mathbf{D}} - 2\mathbf{C}$ is skew-symmetric, which is the algebraic statement of energy conservation and underpins almost every passivity-based controller.

At high speed, $\mathbf{h}$ can swallow 20–40% of your actuator budget. Ignore it and your trajectory tracking will drift the moment the arm moves fast.

Why two names for one vector?

Centrifugal = self-coupling ($\dot{q}_j^2$ terms) — a joint reacting on itself. Coriolis = cross-coupling ($\dot{q}_j\dot{q}_k$, $j\ne k$) — two joints moving together generate force on a third. Both live inside the same $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$.

Skew-symmetry → passivity

$\dot{\mathbf{D}}-2\mathbf{C}$ being skew-symmetric is the hook for Lyapunov stability proofs in robot control. It is not a coincidence — it falls out of energy conservation.

4§ 3.4

Gravity terms from potential energy

Of the three terms, gravity is the easiest. The potential energy of the arm is just the sum of $m_i\mathbf{g}^T{}^0\mathbf{r}_{ci}$ over all links, and the gravity vector falls out as $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})=\partial V/\partial\mathbf{q}$:

$$c_i(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{j=i}^{n} m_j\,\mathbf{g}^T\,\mathbf{U}_{ji}\,{}^j\mathbf{r}_{cj}.$$

Note the lower limit: only links outboard of joint $i$ contribute, because joint $i$ does not have to support anything closer to the base than itself. For a large industrial arm, $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ can be 10–50× larger than the dynamic terms during normal operation — which is why gravity compensation is the first feedforward term most controllers implement.

Hold an industrial robot's brake release while powered down — it falls. The torque the brakes were resisting was almost entirely $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$.

Posture matters

A horizontal link has the full moment arm; a vertical link has none. Gravity torque scales with $\sin\theta$ for the link's pitch relative to the gravity axis.

5§ 3.5

Assembling the full dynamic model

Putting all three terms together yields the equation that governs every serial manipulator on Earth:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}}$$

This form is called the inverse dynamics equation — given a desired motion $(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}},\ddot{\mathbf{q}})$ it tells you the torques required. Solving for $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ instead gives forward dynamics, $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{D}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\tau}-\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{c})$, which is what a simulator runs at every time step. For controller design we often linearise around an operating point to get $\mathbf{D}_0\Delta\ddot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{C}_0\Delta\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{K}_0\Delta\mathbf{q}=\Delta\boldsymbol{\tau}$, a linear time-invariant system to which all of classical control theory applies.

Three terms. Two directions: feed the model torques and ask 'how does it move?', or feed it a motion and ask 'what torques do I need?' Both are this same equation, rearranged.

Forward vs inverse dynamics

Inverse (controller): given $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$, find $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ — $O(n)$ with Newton-Euler. Forward (simulator): given $\boldsymbol{\tau}$, find $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ — needs $\mathbf{D}^{-1}$, $O(n^3)$.

6§ 3.6

Worked example — 2-DOF planar arm, end-to-end

Two uniform rods of length $L_i$ and mass $m_i$, joined by revolute joints, moving in the vertical plane. $\theta_1$ is link 1's angle from horizontal; $\theta_2$ is link 2's angle relative to link 1.

Centres of mass:

$$\mathbf{r}_{c1}=\tfrac{L_1}{2}\!\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta_1\\\sin\theta_1\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf{r}_{c2}=\!\begin{pmatrix}L_1\cos\theta_1+\tfrac{L_2}{2}\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\\L_1\sin\theta_1+\tfrac{L_2}{2}\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\end{pmatrix}.$$

Inertia matrix (after expanding $T$ and matching the quadratic form):

$$\begin{aligned}D_{11}&=m_1\tfrac{L_1^2}{4}+I_1+m_2\!\left[L_1^2+\tfrac{L_2^2}{4}+L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\right]+I_2,\\D_{12}&=D_{21}=m_2\!\left[\tfrac{L_2^2}{4}+\tfrac{L_1L_2}{2}\cos\theta_2\right]+I_2,\\D_{22}&=m_2\tfrac{L_2^2}{4}+I_2.\end{aligned}$$

Coriolis/centrifugal:

$$h_1=-m_2L_1L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot{\theta}_1\dot{\theta}_2-\tfrac{m_2L_1L_2\sin\theta_2}{2}\dot{\theta}_2^2,\quad h_2=\tfrac{m_2L_1L_2\sin\theta_2}{2}\dot{\theta}_1^2.$$

Gravity:

$$c_1=\!\left(m_1\tfrac{L_1}{2}+m_2L_1\right)\!g\cos\theta_1+m_2g\tfrac{L_2}{2}\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2),\quad c_2=m_2g\tfrac{L_2}{2}\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2).$$

Three observations to take with you: the coupling term $L_1L_2\cos\theta_2$ vanishes at $\theta_2=\pm\pi/2$ (joints momentarily decouple inertially); the Coriolis terms are largest when $\sin\theta_2$ is largest; and even this tiny arm already displays the full zoo of nonlinearity, coupling, and configuration dependence.

Two rods. Two joints. Six pages of algebra. Welcome to robot dynamics — and remember, every additional DOF multiplies the cross-coupling terms.

Decoupling configurations

At $\theta_2=\pm 90°$ the inertial coupling $D_{12}$ collapses to its constant part $m_2L_2^2/4+I_2$ — the kinematic coupling persists, but the configuration-dependent coupling is zero.

The final 2-DOF model in matrix form (MATLAB)
% q = [theta1; theta2], qd = [theta1d; theta2d]
D = [ m1*L1^2/4 + I1 + m2*(L1^2 + L2^2/4 + L1*L2*cos(q(2))) + I2,  ...
      m2*(L2^2/4 + L1*L2/2*cos(q(2))) + I2;
      m2*(L2^2/4 + L1*L2/2*cos(q(2))) + I2,                       ...
      m2*L2^2/4 + I2 ];

h = [ -m2*L1*L2*sin(q(2)) * qd(1)*qd(2) ...
      - m2*L1*L2*sin(q(2))/2 * qd(2)^2;
        m2*L1*L2*sin(q(2))/2 * qd(1)^2 ];

c = [ (m1*L1/2 + m2*L1)*g*cos(q(1)) + m2*g*L2/2*cos(q(1)+q(2));
                                       m2*g*L2/2*cos(q(1)+q(2)) ];

tau = D*qdd + h + c;

7§ 3.7

Computational cost and real-time implementation

A naive evaluation of the Lagrangian formulas costs $O(n^4)$ for the inertia matrix alone — fine for symbolic derivation, painful at a 4 kHz control loop. Two big tricks make this practical:

1. Recursive Newton-Euler for inverse dynamics drops the cost to $O(n)$ — linear in the number of joints. Forward dynamics still needs $\mathbf{D}^{-1}$ at $O(n^3)$, but Cholesky decomposition exploits the symmetry and positive-definiteness of $\mathbf{D}$.

2. Symbolic preprocessing. Derive the equations once in a CAS (MATLAB Symbolic, Mathematica, SymPy), simplify, and emit hand-tuned C code per robot. Industrial controllers (KUKA KR QUANTEC, ABB IRB 6700, FANUC M-20iD) all do some variant of this to hit kHz-rate gravity and Coriolis compensation.

Near kinematic singularities $\mathbf{D}$ becomes ill-conditioned — practitioners add a small regulariser $\lambda\mathbf{I}$, switch to an SVD-based pseudo-inverse, or project onto the feasible motion subspace.

Lagrangian for understanding. Newton-Euler for shipping.

Cost summary

Direct Lagrangian: $O(n^4)$ · Recursive Newton-Euler inverse: $O(n)$ · Forward dynamics: $O(n^3)$ (matrix inversion dominated). Symbolic preprocessing closes most of the constant-factor gap.

Practice — try these on your own

Small activities to test your grip on today's ideas. Try each one first, then reveal the worked solution to compare. These are formative — not graded, not a hand-in.

1 · Derive $D_{22}$ for the 2-DOF arm from scratch

Starting from link 2's velocity expression $\mathbf{v}_{c2}$ given in the lecture, isolate the coefficient of $\dot{\theta}_2^2$ in the total kinetic energy and show that

$$D_{22} = m_2\,\frac{L_2^2}{4} + I_2.$$

Do not skip the rotational kinetic energy term $\tfrac{1}{2}I_2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2$ — it contributes too.

Self-check after you've tried

Your answer should contain no $\theta$-dependence (unlike $D_{11}$ and $D_{12}$) · units must be kg·m² · setting $I_2=0$ should leave the moment of inertia of a point mass $m_2$ at distance $L_2/2$.

Show worked solution

Group the velocity of link 2's COM by which joint rate it depends on:

$$\mathbf{v}_{c2} = \mathbf{J}_1\dot{\theta}_1 + \mathbf{J}_2\dot{\theta}_2,\qquad \mathbf{J}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac{L_2}{2}\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\\\tfrac{L_2}{2}\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\end{bmatrix}.$$

The translational KE of link 2 is $\tfrac{1}{2}m_2\mathbf{v}_{c2}^T\mathbf{v}_{c2}$. The coefficient of $\dot{\theta}_2^2$ comes only from $\mathbf{J}_2^T\mathbf{J}_2$:

$$\mathbf{J}_2^T\mathbf{J}_2 = \tfrac{L_2^2}{4}\!\left[\sin^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)+\cos^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)\right] = \tfrac{L_2^2}{4}.$$

So the translational contribution to $D_{22}$ is $m_2\,L_2^2/4$. Now the rotational KE of link 2 is $\tfrac{1}{2}I_2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2$; the coefficient of $\dot{\theta}_2^2$ here is $I_2$. Adding:

$$\boxed{D_{22} = m_2\,\frac{L_2^2}{4} + I_2.}$$

Notice the result is independent of configuration — link 2's own inertia about its own joint does not change as the arm folds. That is why $D_{22}$ alone is constant while $D_{11}$ and $D_{12}$ carry $\cos\theta_2$ terms.

2 · Compute the Christoffel symbol $\Gamma_{122}$

Using the inertia matrix elements $D_{11}, D_{12}, D_{22}$ from the lecture, evaluate

$$\Gamma_{122} = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\tfrac{\partial D_{12}}{\partial q_2} + \tfrac{\partial D_{12}}{\partial q_2} - \tfrac{\partial D_{22}}{\partial q_1}\right).$$

Show that the answer is $-\tfrac{m_2 L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2}{2}$, and explain in one sentence why the $\partial D_{22}/\partial q_1$ term vanishes.

Self-check after you've tried

Sign should be negative (it appears as $-\sin\theta_2$) · result should vanish at $\theta_2 = 0$ and $\theta_2 = \pi$ · only $D_{12}$ contributes — $D_{22}$ derivative is zero.

Show worked solution

Take the three derivatives one by one.

Derivative 1: $\frac{\partial D_{12}}{\partial q_2} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_2}\!\left[m_2\!\left(\tfrac{L_2^2}{4}+\tfrac{L_1L_2}{2}\cos\theta_2\right)+I_2\right] = -\tfrac{m_2 L_1 L_2}{2}\sin\theta_2.$

Derivative 2: same as Derivative 1 (the formula has $\partial D_{12}/\partial q_2$ twice when $i=1, j=k=2$).

Derivative 3: $\frac{\partial D_{22}}{\partial q_1} = 0$, because $D_{22}=m_2 L_2^2/4 + I_2$ contains no $\theta_1$ dependence. Physically: rotating link 1 as a rigid unit with link 2 does not change link 2's inertia about its own joint.

Assembling:

$$\Gamma_{122} = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(-\tfrac{m_2L_1L_2}{2}\sin\theta_2 \;-\;\tfrac{m_2L_1L_2}{2}\sin\theta_2 \;-\; 0\right) = \boxed{-\tfrac{m_2L_1L_2}{2}\sin\theta_2.}$$

This matches equation (3.43) in the lecture and is the Christoffel symbol that drives the Coriolis term $h_1$.

3 · Find the configurations where the inertial coupling vanishes

For the 2-DOF planar arm, find every value of $\theta_2 \in [0, 2\pi)$ at which the configuration-dependent part of $D_{12}$ — that is, the $\cos\theta_2$ term — equals zero. Then sketch (in words is fine) what the arm looks like at those configurations, and explain physically why the inertial coupling between the two joints disappears there.

Self-check after you've tried

Two answers in $[0,2\pi)$ · both should be quarter-turn offsets · at these poses, the link-2 COM moves perpendicular to the line from joint 1 to itself when joint 1 rotates.

Show worked solution

Setting $\cos\theta_2 = 0$ gives $\theta_2 = \pi/2$ or $\theta_2 = 3\pi/2$ — link 2 sticks out at a right angle to link 1, either to the 'left' or to the 'right'.

At these configurations $D_{12}$ collapses to its constant residual $m_2 L_2^2/4 + I_2$, the inertia coming purely from link 2's own moment about its own joint. The full configuration-driven coupling is gone.

Physical picture. When joint 1 alone rotates with $\theta_2=\pi/2$, the COM of link 2 sweeps along an arc whose instantaneous velocity is exactly perpendicular to the radial direction from joint 1. Because of this geometric arrangement, an angular acceleration at joint 1 produces no tangential push on the line connecting joint 1 to link 2's COM, and so no extra reaction torque at joint 2 beyond link 2's own rotational inertia. Geometry has momentarily decoupled the two joints — for that instant only. The moment $\theta_2$ moves off $\pi/2$, the cosine wakes up again and the coupling returns.

4 · Verify skew-symmetry on the 2-DOF arm

Pick the simplest non-trivial state: $\theta_2 = \pi/4$, $\dot{\theta}_1 = 1$ rad/s, $\dot{\theta}_2 = 0$. Form the Coriolis matrix $\mathbf{C}$ from the Christoffel symbols and compute $\dot{\mathbf{D}}$ directly from $D_{jk}(\theta_2)$ using the chain rule (only $\theta_2$ appears, so $\dot{D}_{jk}=\frac{\partial D_{jk}}{\partial \theta_2}\dot{\theta}_2$ — but wait!). Then verify that the matrix $\dot{\mathbf{D}} - 2\mathbf{C}$ is skew-symmetric.

Hint: $\dot{\theta}_2 = 0$ is a trap; the relation must hold for the general velocity state, so use symbolic $\dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2$ and check the algebra.

Self-check after you've tried

$(\dot{\mathbf{D}}-2\mathbf{C})^T = -(\dot{\mathbf{D}}-2\mathbf{C})$ means the diagonal entries are zero and the (1,2) entry is minus the (2,1) entry · result must hold for all $\dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2$, not just one numerical state · if your diagonal is nonzero, you wrote $\mathbf{C}$ with the wrong index convention.

Show worked solution

Let $s = \sin\theta_2$ and abbreviate $\alpha = m_2 L_1 L_2$. The configuration-dependent parts of $\mathbf{D}$ are:

$$D_{11}\supset \alpha\cos\theta_2,\quad D_{12}=D_{21}\supset \tfrac{\alpha}{2}\cos\theta_2,\quad D_{22}=\text{const}.$$

Differentiating in time (only $\theta_2$ varies):

$$\dot{\mathbf{D}} = -\alpha\,s\,\dot{\theta}_2\begin{bmatrix}1 & \tfrac{1}{2}\\ \tfrac{1}{2} & 0\end{bmatrix}.$$

From the Christoffel symbols computed earlier ($\Gamma_{122}=\Gamma_{212}=-\tfrac{\alpha s}{2}$, $\Gamma_{211}=\tfrac{\alpha s}{2}$, all others zero), and using $C_{ij}=\sum_k \Gamma_{ikj}\dot{q}_k$:

$$\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix}C_{11}&C_{12}\\ C_{21}&C_{22}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac{\alpha s}{2}\dot{\theta}_2 & -\tfrac{\alpha s}{2}(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)\\ \tfrac{\alpha s}{2}\dot{\theta}_1 & 0\end{bmatrix}.$$

Now compute $\mathbf{N} = \dot{\mathbf{D}} - 2\mathbf{C}$:

  • $N_{11} = -\alpha s\dot{\theta}_2 - 2(-\tfrac{\alpha s}{2}\dot{\theta}_2) = 0.$ ✓
  • $N_{22} = 0 - 0 = 0.$ ✓
  • $N_{12} = -\tfrac{\alpha s}{2}\dot{\theta}_2 - 2(-\tfrac{\alpha s}{2}(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)) = -\tfrac{\alpha s}{2}\dot{\theta}_2 + \alpha s(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2) = \alpha s\dot{\theta}_1 + \tfrac{\alpha s}{2}\dot{\theta}_2.$
  • $N_{21} = -\tfrac{\alpha s}{2}\dot{\theta}_2 - 2(\tfrac{\alpha s}{2}\dot{\theta}_1) = -\alpha s\dot{\theta}_1 - \tfrac{\alpha s}{2}\dot{\theta}_2.$

Clearly $N_{21} = -N_{12}$ and the diagonal is zero, so

$$\boxed{\dot{\mathbf{D}} - 2\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix}0 & \alpha s(\dot{\theta}_1+\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\theta}_2)\\ -\alpha s(\dot{\theta}_1+\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\theta}_2) & 0\end{bmatrix}}$$

is skew-symmetric for all $\dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2$ — exactly as the Euler-Lagrange theory promises. This is not a coincidence; it is energy conservation expressed in matrix form, and it is the structural property that makes Lyapunov-based robot controllers possible.
