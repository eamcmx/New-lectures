Lecture 05 · Module 3 · Robot Manipulator Dynamics

Lagrangian Formulation for Robot Dynamics

From link velocities to the full $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ equation.

Today we build the complete dynamic model of a robot manipulator from the ground up, the Lagrangian way. We start with how velocities propagate through the kinematic chain, turn that into kinetic energy, pull the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ straight out of it, then chase the velocity-dependent Coriolis and centrifugal terms $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ and the gravitational vector $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ out of potential energy. By the end you should be able to write down $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{c} = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ for any serial arm — and we'll prove it on a 2-DOF planar example.

§ 0

What this lecture covers

This module assembles the complete robot dynamic equation $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ piece by piece, using the Lagrangian (energy-based) approach.

We will: (1) express link velocities via transformation-matrix derivatives and the influence coefficients $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$; (2) derive the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ from total kinetic energy; (3) extract the Coriolis and centrifugal vector $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ via Christoffel symbols; (4) compute the gravitational vector $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ from potential energy; (5) assemble everything and apply it to a worked 2-DOF planar arm.

We close with computational-complexity notes — direct evaluation is $O(n^4)$, recursive Newton–Euler is $O(n)$ — and how real industrial controllers exploit that.

Think of this as the bridge between where the robot is and what torque each motor must produce. Get the energy bookkeeping right and the equations of motion fall out almost mechanically.

Prerequisites

Comfort with homogeneous transformations ${}^{j-1}\mathbf{A}_j$, partial derivatives, and the Euler–Lagrange equation $\tfrac{d}{dt}(\partial T/\partial \dot q_i) - \partial T/\partial q_i = \tau_i$.

By the end you can…

Hand-derive $\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{h}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ for a 2-DOF or 3-DOF planar arm, and write the same procedure as an algorithm for $n$ joints.

§ 1

Velocity formulation via transformation matrices

All robot dynamics starts with one fact: when joint $j$ moves, it shakes every link further out the chain. We need a systematic way to write that.

A point fixed in link $i$ has base-frame position

$${}^0\mathbf{r}_i = {}^0\mathbf{A}_1\,{}^1\mathbf{A}_2 \cdots {}^{i-1}\mathbf{A}_i\,{}^i\mathbf{r}_i. \tag{3.1}$$

Differentiating in time, and remembering that ${}^i\mathbf{r}_i$ is constant in its own frame, gives

$${}^0\mathbf{v}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{i} \frac{\partial\, {}^0\mathbf{A}_i}{\partial q_j}\,\dot{q}_j\,{}^i\mathbf{r}_i. \tag{3.3}$$

The partial derivative is not something we evaluate symbolically every time. Instead we use a constant 'joint-type' matrix $\mathbf{Q}_j$ (one for revolute, one for prismatic) and the identity

$$\frac{\partial\, {}^0\mathbf{A}_i}{\partial q_j} = {}^0\mathbf{A}_j\,\mathbf{Q}_j\,{}^j\mathbf{A}_i. \tag{3.6}$$

Defining the velocity influence coefficient $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$,

$$\mathbf{U}_{ij} = \begin{cases}{}^0\mathbf{A}_j\,\mathbf{Q}_j\,{}^j\mathbf{A}_i & j\le i \\ \mathbf{0} & j>i \end{cases} \tag{3.7}$$

we get the compact form

$${}^0\mathbf{v}_i = \Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{i} \mathbf{U}_{ij}\,\dot{q}_j\Bigr)\,{}^i\mathbf{r}_i. \tag{3.8}$$

For the kinetic-energy work that follows we also need how $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$ itself changes with another joint $k$:

$$\mathbf{U}_{ijk} = \frac{\partial\mathbf{U}_{ij}}{\partial q_k},$$

which expands into three cases depending on the ordering of $i,j,k$ along the chain (eq. 3.9).

The $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$ are bookkeeping. Once you accept them, every later step is just plugging in.

The joint-type matrix $\mathbf{Q}_j$

For a revolute joint $\mathbf{Q}_j$ is a 4×4 with a 2×2 skew rotation in the top-left block; for a prismatic joint it has a single 1 in the (3,4) position. Either way, $\mathbf{Q}_j$ encodes 'this joint's infinitesimal screw motion'.

Causality of the chain

$\mathbf{U}_{ij}=\mathbf{0}$ whenever $j>i$: joints further out the chain than link $i$ cannot move link $i$. That's where the lower-triangular structure of robot dynamics comes from.

Try it · velocity cascade

Pick a joint to wiggle and watch the velocity influence coefficient $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$ ripple along every outboard link. Each stage builds one factor of ${}^0\mathbf{A}_j\,\mathbf{Q}_j\,{}^j\mathbf{A}_i$ so you can see why joints further out the chain can't move inboard links.

§ 2

Kinetic energy and the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$

Each link carries kinetic energy that depends quadratically on the joint velocities. Summing over all $n$ links and using the influence coefficients $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$,

$$T = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\sum_{j,k}\mathbf{U}_{ij}\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{U}_{ik}^{T}\dot q_j\dot q_k\right], \tag{3.10}$$

where $\mathbf{J}_i$ is the inertia tensor of link $i$ expressed in the base frame.

Swap the order of summation and compare against the canonical quadratic form $T = \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^{T}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}}$. The elements of $\mathbf{D}$ pop out:

$$\boxed{\,D_{jk}(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\mathbf{U}_{ij}\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{U}_{ik}^{T}\bigr).\,} \tag{3.12}$$

This is the workhorse formula. Each $D_{jk}$ measures how strongly joints $j$ and $k$ are coupled through every link that they both influence.

If you remember only one equation from today, make it (3.12). Every property of $\mathbf{D}$ — symmetry, positive-definiteness, configuration dependence — is read straight off it.

Properties of $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$

Symmetric ($D_{jk}=D_{kj}$, from the trace). Positive-definite (kinetic energy is always positive for nonzero $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$). Configuration-dependent (the $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$ contain transformation matrices). Diagonal entries = effective inertia at joint $j$; off-diagonals = inertial coupling.

Algorithm 3.1 — compute $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$

Initialise $\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{0}$. For each link $i=1\dots n$: build ${}^0\mathbf{A}_1,\dots,{}^0\mathbf{A}_i$; for every pair $(j,k)$ with $j,k\le i$, accumulate $D_{jk}\mathrel{+}=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{U}_{ij}\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{U}_{ik}^{T})$. Finally enforce $D_{kj}=D_{jk}$.

§ 3

Coriolis and centrifugal terms $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$

Because $\mathbf{D}$ depends on $\mathbf{q}$, taking the Euler–Lagrange time derivative of $\partial T/\partial\dot q_i$ produces velocity-product terms in addition to $\sum_j D_{ij}\ddot q_j$. Collecting them, and subtracting $\partial T/\partial q_i$, the velocity-dependent part is

$$h_i(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) = \sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{ijk}\,\dot{q}_j\dot{q}_k, \tag{3.17}$$

with the Christoffel symbols of the first kind

$$\Gamma_{ijk} = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\frac{\partial D_{ij}}{\partial q_k} + \frac{\partial D_{ik}}{\partial q_j} - \frac{\partial D_{jk}}{\partial q_i}\right). \tag{3.16}$$

Equivalently $\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ with $C_{ij}=\sum_k\Gamma_{ikj}\dot q_k$ (eq. 3.19).

Physically, terms with $\dot q_j\dot q_k$ for $j\ne k$ are Coriolis (two joints moving together); terms with $\dot q_j^2$ are centrifugal (one joint's motion reacting on itself through the changing inertia).

At low speed you can ignore $\mathbf{h}$ entirely. At industrial-robot speeds it can eat 20–40% of your motor torque, so you cannot.

Passivity property

$\dot{\mathbf{D}}(\mathbf{q}) - 2\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ is skew-symmetric (eq. 3.20). This isn't a curiosity — it's the cornerstone of every Lyapunov stability proof for robot control.

Algorithm 3.2 — compute $\mathbf{h}$

From $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$, evaluate the partials $\partial D_{jk}/\partial q_i$ (analytically or numerically); assemble the Christoffel symbols; then $h_i = \sum_{j,k}\Gamma_{ijk}\dot q_j\dot q_k$.

Now you build $h_1$ and $h_2$ from scratch. At each step you pick which production rule to apply — the Christoffel definition, a partial-derivative substitution, a vanish-by-zero rule — and the widget expands the form until you've recovered equations (3.45)–(3.46).

§ 4

Gravitational terms $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$

The gravitational terms are the easy part of the model — they come from one scalar, the potential energy:

$$V = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\,\mathbf{g}^{T}\,{}^0\mathbf{r}_{ci}, \tag{3.21}$$

where $\mathbf{g} = [0,0,-g,0]^T$ in homogeneous coordinates and ${}^0\mathbf{r}_{ci}$ is the centre of mass of link $i$ in the base frame.

Differentiating with respect to each joint coordinate gives

$$c_i(\mathbf{q}) = \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_i} = \sum_{j=i}^{n} m_j\,\mathbf{g}^{T}\,\mathbf{U}_{ji}\,{}^j\mathbf{r}_{cj}. \tag{3.25}$$

Note the sum starts at $j=i$: a joint only feels the weight of links outboard of itself. The same $\mathbf{U}_{ij}$ machinery we built for velocities is reused — that's the elegance of the Lagrangian approach.

For a heavy industrial arm, gravity loading can be 10–50× larger than the dynamic terms during normal motion. Most controllers add a gravity-compensation feedforward purely from $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$.

Configuration dependence

Vertical link → ~0 gravitational torque (gravity acts along the link, no moment arm). Horizontal link → maximum torque. In between, torque scales like $\sin\theta$.

Algorithm 3.3 — compute $\mathbf{c}$

For each joint $i$, sum over outboard links $j\ge i$: $c_i \mathrel{+}= m_j\,\mathbf{g}^{T}\mathbf{U}_{ji}\,{}^j\mathbf{r}_{cj}$. Cheap, only $O(n^2)$.

§ 5

Assembling the complete dynamic equation

Putting $\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{h}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ together yields the canonical robot equation of motion:

$$\boxed{\,\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\,\ddot{\mathbf{q}} \;+\; \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) \;+\; \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) \;=\; \boldsymbol{\tau}\,.\,} \tag{3.26}$$

This one equation supports two operating modes:

  • Inverse dynamics (given motion, find torque): plug $\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}},\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ in directly — the workhorse of feedforward control.
  • Forward dynamics (given torque, predict motion): $$\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1}(\mathbf{q})\bigl[\boldsymbol{\tau} - \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) - \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})\bigr]. \tag{3.28}$$ Integrate twice and you have a simulator.

The full model is nonlinear, coupled, and configuration-dependent — but every term has a clean physical meaning: inertia × acceleration, velocity products, gravity, applied torque.

This is the equation that sits inside every robot simulator and every model-based controller on the planet.

Model properties to remember

$\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}^T$, $\mathbf{D}>0$, $\dot{\mathbf{D}}-2\mathbf{C}$ skew-symmetric. All terms bounded for finite $\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}$. These four facts are enough to build a stable passivity-based controller.

Linearised form

Around a working point $(\mathbf{q}_0,\dot{\mathbf{q}}_0)$ the equations reduce to $\mathbf{D}_0\Delta\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{C}_0\Delta\dot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{K}_0\Delta\mathbf{q} = \Delta\boldsymbol{\tau}$ — i.e. a mass-spring-damper, which is what linear control design assumes.

§ 6

Worked example — 2-DOF planar arm

Consider a planar arm with two revolute joints. Each link is a uniform rod with length $L_i$, mass $m_i$, central moment of inertia $I_i$. Joint angles: $\theta_1$ from the horizontal, $\theta_2$ relative to link 1.

Step 1 — centre-of-mass positions.

$$\mathbf{r}_{c1}=\tfrac{L_1}{2}[\cos\theta_1,\sin\theta_1]^T,$$ $$\mathbf{r}_{c2}=[L_1\cos\theta_1 + \tfrac{L_2}{2}\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2),\;L_1\sin\theta_1 + \tfrac{L_2}{2}\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)]^T.$$

Step 2 — velocities. Differentiate; $\mathbf{v}_{c2}$ splits cleanly into a piece driven by $\dot\theta_1$ and a piece driven by $\dot\theta_2$.

Step 3 — kinetic energy. $T = T_1 + T_2$ with $T_i = \tfrac{1}{2}m_i\mathbf{v}_{ci}^T\mathbf{v}_{ci} + \tfrac{1}{2}I_i\omega_i^2$. After expansion this rearranges into the quadratic form $T = \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^T\mathbf{D}\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ with

$$\begin{aligned} D_{11} &= m_1\tfrac{L_1^2}{4} + I_1 + m_2\Bigl[L_1^2 + \tfrac{L_2^2}{4} + L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\Bigr] + I_2,\\ D_{12} &= D_{21} = m_2\Bigl[\tfrac{L_2^2}{4} + \tfrac{L_1L_2}{2}\cos\theta_2\Bigr] + I_2,\\ D_{22} &= m_2\tfrac{L_2^2}{4} + I_2. \end{aligned}$$

Step 4 — potential energy.

$$V = m_1 g\tfrac{L_1}{2}\sin\theta_1 + m_2 g\Bigl(L_1\sin\theta_1 + \tfrac{L_2}{2}\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\Bigr).$$

Step 5 — Coriolis/centrifugal. Only $\cos\theta_2$ terms have nonzero partial derivatives, so

$$h_1 = -m_2 L_1 L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2 - \tfrac{m_2 L_1 L_2\sin\theta_2}{2}\dot\theta_2^{\,2},$$ $$h_2 = \tfrac{m_2 L_1 L_2\sin\theta_2}{2}\dot\theta_1^{\,2}.$$

Step 6 — gravity.

$$c_1 = m_1 g\tfrac{L_1}{2}\cos\theta_1 + m_2 g L_1\cos\theta_1 + m_2 g\tfrac{L_2}{2}\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2),$$ $$c_2 = m_2 g\tfrac{L_2}{2}\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2).$$

Step 7 — complete model. Stack into $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{c} = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ — done.

Notice how every nonlinearity in the 2-DOF model is driven by a single quantity: $\cos\theta_2$ in the inertia, $\sin\theta_2$ in the Coriolis terms. That's the geometric coupling of the elbow showing up.

Sanity checks

When $\theta_2 = 90°$, $\cos\theta_2=0$ and the $\cos\theta_2$ terms of the inertia matrix vanish — the geometric coupling disappears (the constant $m_2 L_2^2/4 + I_2$ piece in $D_{12}$ remains). When $\theta_2 = 0$ (arm straight), $\sin\theta_2=0$ and Coriolis forces vanish entirely. Useful for debugging your derivation.

Watch the cross-term

When you expand $(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2$ in $T_2$, group like terms carefully — the $L_1L_2\cos\theta_2$ coefficient is the only true coupling source. Don't double-count an extra $L_2^2/2$ piece.

Inertia and torque vector for the 2-DOF arm (symbolic-style) matlab 
% Inputs: th1, th2, dth1, dth2, ddth1, ddth2, L1, L2, m1, m2, I1, I2, g

D11 = m1*L1^2/4 + I1 + m2*(L1^2 + L2^2/4 + L1*L2*cos(th2)) + I2;
D12 = m2*(L2^2/4 + L1*L2/2*cos(th2)) + I2;
D21 = D12;
D22 = m2*L2^2/4 + I2;

h1  = -m2*L1*L2*sin(th2)*dth1*dth2 - m2*L1*L2*sin(th2)/2*dth2^2;
h2  =  m2*L1*L2*sin(th2)/2*dth1^2;

c1  = m1*g*L1/2*cos(th1) + m2*g*L1*cos(th1) + m2*g*L2/2*cos(th1+th2);
c2  = m2*g*L2/2*cos(th1+th2);

tau1 = D11*ddth1 + D12*ddth2 + h1 + c1;
tau2 = D21*ddth1 + D22*ddth2 + h2 + c2;

§ 7

Computational considerations

The Lagrangian formulation is mathematically clean but expensive: direct evaluation of (3.12) costs $O(n^4)$ floating-point operations per call. For 6-DOF arms running at 1–4 kHz control rates that's painful.

Recursive Newton–Euler algorithms compute the same torques in $O(n)$ for inverse dynamics and $O(n^3)$ for forward dynamics — orders of magnitude cheaper. Most real controllers use Newton–Euler for online torque computation and reserve the Lagrangian form for derivation, analysis, and symbolic code generation.

Implementation tricks that work in practice:

  1. Symbolic preprocessing — derive equations once in MATLAB Symbolic Toolbox or SymPy, then auto-generate C code.
  2. Cholesky factorisation of $\mathbf{D}$ (it's symmetric positive-definite) instead of general LU for $\mathbf{D}^{-1}$.
  3. Regularising $\mathbf{D}\to\mathbf{D}+\lambda\mathbf{I}$ near kinematic singularities.
  4. Precomputing $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ lookup tables when configurations are limited.

Algorithm choice is a control-rate problem. If you need 4 kHz, you cannot afford an $O(n^4)$ inner loop — choose recursion or codegen accordingly.

Self-assessed activities

Small activities to test your grip on today's ideas. Try each one first, then reveal the worked solution to compare.

1 · Verify the symmetry of $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$

Using the definition $D_{jk}(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{U}_{ij}\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{U}_{ik}^{T})$ from equation (3.12), show directly that $D_{jk}=D_{kj}$ for all $j,k$. You may use any standard property of the trace, and you should state which property you are invoking.

Hint: what does $\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})$ equal when $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are conformable, and how does $\mathbf{J}_i$ relate to $\mathbf{J}_i^T$?

Self-check after you've tried
  • Your proof should be 3–6 lines long.
  • You must use BOTH the cyclic property $\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{AB})=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{BA})$ AND the symmetry $\mathbf{J}_i = \mathbf{J}_i^T$.
  • Final line: clearly state $D_{jk}=D_{kj}$.
Show worked solution

Claim: $D_{jk}(\mathbf{q}) = D_{kj}(\mathbf{q})$ for all $j,k$.

Proof. Start from

$$D_{jk} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\mathbf{U}_{ij}\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{U}_{ik}^{T}\bigr).$$

For each $i$, take the transpose of the matrix inside the trace. Using $(\mathbf{ABC})^T = \mathbf{C}^T\mathbf{B}^T\mathbf{A}^T$,

$$\bigl(\mathbf{U}_{ij}\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{U}_{ik}^{T}\bigr)^{T} = \mathbf{U}_{ik}\,\mathbf{J}_i^{T}\,\mathbf{U}_{ij}^{T}.$$

The inertia tensor of a rigid body is symmetric, so $\mathbf{J}_i^{T} = \mathbf{J}_i$. Therefore

$$\bigl(\mathbf{U}_{ij}\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{U}_{ik}^{T}\bigr)^{T} = \mathbf{U}_{ik}\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{U}_{ij}^{T}.$$

The trace is invariant under transposition ($\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{M})=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{M}^T)$), so

$$\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\mathbf{U}_{ij}\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{U}_{ik}^{T}\bigr) = \operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\mathbf{U}_{ik}\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{U}_{ij}^{T}\bigr).$$

Summing over $i$ from $1$ to $n$,

$$D_{jk} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\mathbf{U}_{ik}\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{U}_{ij}^{T}\bigr) = D_{kj}. \quad\blacksquare$$

Take-away: The symmetry of $\mathbf{D}$ is a direct consequence of the symmetry of each link's inertia tensor. We didn't need to know anything about the kinematics.

2 · Christoffel symbols for the 2-DOF arm

For the 2-DOF planar arm from the worked example, the only configuration-dependent inertia entries are

$$D_{11} = \alpha + 2\beta\cos\theta_2,\qquad D_{12} = D_{21} = \delta + \beta\cos\theta_2,\qquad D_{22} = \delta,$$

where $\alpha,\beta,\delta$ are constants you can read off the worked solution. Compute all Christoffel symbols $\Gamma_{ijk}$ ($i,j,k\in\{1,2\}$), then use them to reconstruct $h_1$ and $h_2$. Verify your answer matches equations (3.45)–(3.46).

Self-check after you've tried
  • You should evaluate 8 symbols in total (2×2×2), most of which vanish.
  • The only nonzero partial derivatives are $\partial D_{11}/\partial\theta_2$ and $\partial D_{12}/\partial\theta_2$.
  • After summing $h_i = \sum_{j,k}\Gamma_{ijk}\dot\theta_j\dot\theta_k$ you should recover $h_2 = +\tfrac{1}{2}m_2 L_1 L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1^{\,2}$.
Show worked solution

Identify the parameters. From the worked example,

$$\alpha = m_1\tfrac{L_1^2}{4} + I_1 + m_2 L_1^2 + m_2\tfrac{L_2^2}{4} + I_2,\quad \beta = \tfrac{m_2 L_1 L_2}{2},\quad \delta = m_2\tfrac{L_2^2}{4} + I_2.$$

Step 1 — required partials. With $D_{22}$ constant and only $\theta_2$ appearing in $D_{11},D_{12}$:

$$\frac{\partial D_{11}}{\partial\theta_1} = 0,\qquad \frac{\partial D_{11}}{\partial\theta_2} = -2\beta\sin\theta_2,$$ $$\frac{\partial D_{12}}{\partial\theta_1} = 0,\qquad \frac{\partial D_{12}}{\partial\theta_2} = -\beta\sin\theta_2,$$ $$\frac{\partial D_{22}}{\partial\theta_1} = \frac{\partial D_{22}}{\partial\theta_2} = 0.$$

Step 2 — apply $\Gamma_{ijk} = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\partial D_{ij}/\partial q_k + \partial D_{ik}/\partial q_j - \partial D_{jk}/\partial q_i\right)$.

For $i=1$:

  • $\Gamma_{111} = \tfrac{1}{2}(0+0-0) = 0$.
  • $\Gamma_{112} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\partial D_{11}/\partial\theta_2 + \partial D_{12}/\partial\theta_1 - \partial D_{12}/\partial\theta_1) = \tfrac{1}{2}(-2\beta\sin\theta_2) = -\beta\sin\theta_2$.
  • $\Gamma_{121} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\partial D_{12}/\partial\theta_1 + \partial D_{11}/\partial\theta_2 - \partial D_{21}/\partial\theta_1) = \tfrac{1}{2}(0 + (-2\beta\sin\theta_2) - 0) = -\beta\sin\theta_2$.
  • $\Gamma_{122} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\partial D_{12}/\partial\theta_2 + \partial D_{12}/\partial\theta_2 - \partial D_{22}/\partial\theta_1) = \tfrac{1}{2}(-\beta\sin\theta_2 - \beta\sin\theta_2 - 0) = -\beta\sin\theta_2$.

For $i=2$:

  • $\Gamma_{211} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\partial D_{21}/\partial\theta_1 + \partial D_{21}/\partial\theta_1 - \partial D_{11}/\partial\theta_2) = \tfrac{1}{2}(0 + 0 - (-2\beta\sin\theta_2)) = +\beta\sin\theta_2$.
  • $\Gamma_{212} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\partial D_{21}/\partial\theta_2 + \partial D_{22}/\partial\theta_1 - \partial D_{12}/\partial\theta_2) = \tfrac{1}{2}(-\beta\sin\theta_2 + 0 - (-\beta\sin\theta_2)) = 0$.
  • $\Gamma_{221} = \Gamma_{212} = 0$ (by the symmetry $\Gamma_{ijk}=\Gamma_{ikj}$ in the lower indices, which you can verify from the formula).
  • $\Gamma_{222} = \tfrac{1}{2}(0+0-0) = 0$.

Step 3 — assemble $\mathbf{h}$. Using $h_i = \sum_{j,k}\Gamma_{ijk}\dot\theta_j\dot\theta_k$,

$$h_1 = \Gamma_{112}\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2 + \Gamma_{121}\dot\theta_2\dot\theta_1 + \Gamma_{122}\dot\theta_2^{\,2} = -2\beta\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2 - \beta\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2^{\,2}.$$ $$h_2 = \Gamma_{211}\dot\theta_1^{\,2} = +\beta\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1^{\,2}.$$

Step 4 — substitute $\beta = m_2 L_1 L_2/2$:

$$\boxed{h_1 = -m_2 L_1 L_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2 - \tfrac{m_2 L_1 L_2\sin\theta_2}{2}\dot\theta_2^{\,2},\quad h_2 = +\tfrac{m_2 L_1 L_2\sin\theta_2}{2}\dot\theta_1^{\,2}.}$$

These match equations (3.45)–(3.46) exactly.

3 · Static gravity torques at three poses

Take the 2-DOF arm with $L_1=L_2=0.5$ m, $m_1=m_2=2$ kg, $g=9.81$ m/s$^2$. Compute the static joint torques $\tau_1,\tau_2$ (i.e. $\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\ddot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbf{0}$) required to hold the arm in each of three poses:

(a) $\theta_1 = 0$, $\theta_2 = 0$ (fully extended horizontally),
(b) $\theta_1 = 90°$, $\theta_2 = 0$ (fully extended vertically up),
(c) $\theta_1 = 0$, $\theta_2 = 90°$ (link 1 horizontal, link 2 vertical up).

Report numerical values in N·m and comment on the physical reasonableness of each.

Self-check after you've tried
  • At static equilibrium $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ only — $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}$ and $\mathbf{h}$ both vanish.
  • Pose (b): both torques should be zero (gravity along the link, no moment arm). If you don't get exactly 0, recheck the use of $\cos\theta_1$ vs $\sin\theta_1$.
  • Pose (a): $\tau_1$ should be the largest of the three; expect roughly $\tau_1 \approx 12$–$15$ N·m and $\tau_2 \approx 2$–$3$ N·m.
  • Signs: positive $\tau$ means torque resisting gravity-driven collapse.
Show worked solution

Setup. Static $\Rightarrow$ $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$, where

$$c_1 = \bigl(m_1\tfrac{L_1}{2} + m_2 L_1\bigr)g\cos\theta_1 + m_2\tfrac{L_2}{2}g\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2),$$ $$c_2 = m_2\tfrac{L_2}{2}g\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2).$$

With $L_1=L_2=0.5$, $m_1=m_2=2$, $g=9.81$:

  • $(m_1 L_1/2 + m_2 L_1)g = (0.5 + 1.0)\cdot 9.81 = 14.715$.
  • $m_2 (L_2/2)\,g = 2\cdot 0.25\cdot 9.81 = 4.905$.

So

$$c_1 = 14.715\cos\theta_1 + 4.905\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2),\quad c_2 = 4.905\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2).$$

Pose (a): $\theta_1=0,\theta_2=0$ (arm straight, horizontal).

$$\tau_1 = 14.715(1) + 4.905(1) = 19.62\ \text{N·m},\qquad \tau_2 = 4.905(1) = 4.905\ \text{N·m}.$$

Biggest gravity loading, as expected — both links are fully horizontal, so every gram contributes a full moment arm.

Pose (b): $\theta_1=90°,\theta_2=0$ (arm straight, vertical up).

$$\cos 90° = 0,\;\cos(90°+0°)=0\;\Rightarrow\;\tau_1 = 0,\;\tau_2 = 0.$$

Gravity acts along the links — no moment arm, no torque required. Matches the physical intuition.

Pose (c): $\theta_1=0,\theta_2=90°$ (link 1 horizontal, link 2 vertical up).

$$\cos 0=1,\;\cos(0+90°)=0$$ $$\Rightarrow\;\tau_1 = 14.715(1) + 4.905(0) = 14.715\ \text{N·m},\;\tau_2 = 0.$$

Joint 2 sits at the elbow with link 2 straight up — its CoM is directly above the joint axis, so zero torque. Joint 1 still supports both links horizontally; link 2's weight acts along link 1, so it doesn't add to $c_1$, hence the drop from 19.62 to 14.715.

Boxed answers:

$$\boxed{(\mathrm{a})\;\tau_1\!=\!19.62,\,\tau_2\!=\!4.905\;\text{N·m};\;\;(\mathrm{b})\;\tau_1\!=\!\tau_2\!=\!0;\;\;(\mathrm{c})\;\tau_1\!=\!14.715,\,\tau_2\!=\!0\;\text{N·m}.}$$

4 · When does the inertia matrix decouple?

For the 2-DOF arm derived in the worked example, find all values of $\theta_2$ in $[0,2\pi)$ for which the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ becomes diagonal (i.e., $D_{12}=D_{21}=0$). Express your answer in terms of the physical parameters $L_1,L_2,m_2,I_2$. Then, comment briefly on what this means physically about the control problem at those configurations.

Self-check after you've tried
  • You need to solve a single equation in $\theta_2$.
  • The answer should NOT be $\theta_2 = 90°$ unless $I_2 = 0$ — a common mistake. Read $D_{12}$ carefully.
  • If $4I_2 + m_2 L_2^2 > 2m_2 L_1 L_2$ (e.g. when $I_2$ is non-negligible) you may find that no real $\theta_2$ decouples the system.
Show worked solution

Set up the equation. From the worked example,

$$D_{12} = m_2\Bigl[\tfrac{L_2^2}{4} + \tfrac{L_1 L_2}{2}\cos\theta_2\Bigr] + I_2.$$

We want $D_{12} = 0$, i.e.

$$\tfrac{m_2 L_1 L_2}{2}\cos\theta_2 = -\Bigl(\tfrac{m_2 L_2^2}{4} + I_2\Bigr),$$ $$\cos\theta_2 = -\frac{m_2 L_2^2 + 4 I_2}{2 m_2 L_1 L_2}.$$

Feasibility check. Real solutions exist only when the right-hand side lies in $[-1,1]$. Since the numerator is positive, the value is always negative. We need

$$\frac{m_2 L_2^2 + 4 I_2}{2 m_2 L_1 L_2} \le 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m_2 L_2^2 + 4 I_2 \le 2 m_2 L_1 L_2,$$

which rearranges to

$$4 I_2 \le m_2 L_2 (2L_1 - L_2).$$

Case 1 — feasibility holds (typically a 'thin' link 2 with $L_1$ comparable to or larger than $L_2$). There are two decoupling angles in $[0,2\pi)$:

$$\boxed{\;\theta_2^{\ast} = \pm\arccos\!\left(-\frac{m_2 L_2^2 + 4I_2}{2m_2 L_1 L_2}\right) \pmod{2\pi}.\;}$$

Both lie in the second/third quadrants ($\cos$ negative), i.e. with the forearm folded back over the upper arm.

Case 2 — feasibility fails. When $4I_2 > m_2 L_2(2L_1-L_2)$ — common when link 2 is short or has large rotational inertia (motor + gearbox at the elbow) — no real $\theta_2$ decouples the joints. The arm's two joints are always inertially coupled.

Common mis-answer. People often write $\theta_2 = \pi/2$ because $\cos\theta_2 = 0$ kills the $\cos\theta_2$ term. But the $m_2 L_2^2/4 + I_2$ piece is constant and nonzero, so $D_{12}(\pi/2) = m_2 L_2^2/4 + I_2 \neq 0$ in general.

Physical meaning. When (and only when) $\mathbf{D}$ is diagonal, each joint behaves like an independent single-DOF mass: accelerating joint 1 produces no inertial torque on joint 2 and vice versa. At those configurations a decentralised PD controller (independent loop per joint) is the exact feedforward — no cross-coupling compensation needed. Everywhere else, model-based control (computed torque, feedback linearisation) is what buys you tracking accuracy.
