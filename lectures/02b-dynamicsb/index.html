Energy methods & the Lagrangian for robot dynamics

From kinetic and potential energy to the standard equation of motion $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$.

Today we stop drawing free-body diagrams and start counting energy. Newton-Euler works, but with every link you add the bookkeeping of constraint forces and coupled torques explodes. Lagrangian mechanics lets us write down two scalars — kinetic and potential energy — turn a crank, and out fall the full nonlinear equations of motion. Watch how a tangle of joint velocities collapses into a single inertia matrix, and how each term in the final equation tells you something physical about the robot moving in front of you.

What this lecture covers

This module builds the energy-based route to robot dynamics. We contrast force methods (Newton-Euler) with energy methods (Lagrangian) and explain why scalars beat vectors as systems grow. We develop the kinetic and potential energy of a multi-link arm, define the Lagrangian $L = T - V$, and apply the Euler-Lagrange equations to derive the standard form $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$.

By the end you should be able to take any serial manipulator, write its energy, and systematically produce its equations of motion — and recognize the inertia, Coriolis/centrifugal, and gravity terms when you see them.

The whole game: replace vectors with energy, then let calculus do the chain-rule bookkeeping for you.

Learning objectives

Master energy fundamentals in multi-link systems, the Lagrangian framework, generalized coordinates, the Euler-Lagrange equations, and how to systematically derive dynamics for any robot configuration.

1

Why energy methods over force methods?

Both Newton-Euler and Lagrangian mechanics give the same equations — but the path differs enormously. For a single pendulum, force methods are intuitive: draw the free-body diagram, apply $F = ma$. For a multi-link arm with many degrees of freedom, force methods become cumbersome and error-prone.

The fundamental advantage of energy methods is that they are systematic. Instead of tracking individual forces and torques through a kinematic chain, you work with two scalar quantities — kinetic and potential energy — that are easy to compute and combine. The scalar nature removes the coordinate-frame and constraint-force complexity that plagues force-based analysis of multi-body systems.

Constraint forces do real work bookkeeping in Newton-Euler — in Lagrangian mechanics they simply vanish from the final equations.

Force-based (Newton-Euler)

Analyze each link with all forces and torques, including joint constraint forces. Costs: explicit constraint handling, full vector complexity, coupled inter-link forces, and easy sign/coupling errors.

Energy-based (Lagrangian)

Compute total $T$ and $V$, then apply calculus of variations. Wins: scalar quantities, automatic constraint elimination, a well-defined repeatable procedure, and freedom to pick convenient coordinates.

3-DOF arm, two ways

Force method: free-body diagrams for 3 links, 6 balance equations, constraint forces at each joint, coupled ODEs. Energy method: 3 kinetic + 3 potential scalars, form $L = T - V$, apply Euler-Lagrange. Same answer, far fewer pitfalls.

Now you pick a method for a given system and watch the bookkeeping blow up — or stay flat. Choose Newton-Euler and the free-body diagrams, constraint forces and balance equations pile on as links grow; choose Lagrangian and you only ever track two scalars.

2

Kinetic and potential energy in multi-link systems

Kinetic energy in robotics is richer than $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$. Each link has both translational motion (of its center of mass) and rotational motion (about its center of mass), and these are coupled through the kinematic chain: moving joint 1 moves every link beyond it, each with a velocity that depends on all preceding joints.

For a robot with $n$ links the total kinetic energy is

$$T = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ m_i \mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{v}_i + \boldsymbol{\omega}_i^T \mathbf{I}_i \boldsymbol{\omega}_i \right]$$

where both $\mathbf{v}_i$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_i$ depend on the velocities of all joints from the base to link $i$ — the source of the coupling terms.

Potential energy is primarily gravitational and depends only on position:

$$V = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \mathbf{g}^T \mathbf{r}_i$$

It changes dramatically with configuration — an extended horizontal arm stores far more gravitational energy than the same arm folded vertically, which is exactly the gravitational torque the actuators must fight.

Velocity coupling is the whole difficulty: link $i$'s speed is a sum over every joint behind it.

Two motions per link

Each link carries translational KE (center-of-mass motion) plus rotational KE (spin about its center of mass). Sum both, over all links.

Reference frame matters

Potential energy needs a reference height. A smart choice of datum can simplify the algebra significantly. On Earth gravity is $[0,0,-9.81]^T$ m/s², but the framework handles any conservative field.

Drive a 2-DOF arm and watch its energy bars fill in real time: translational KE of each centre of mass, rotational KE about each centre, and gravitational PV rising as the arm lifts. You'll see folded-vertical store little gravity energy while extended-horizontal stores the most.

3

The Lagrangian framework and Euler-Lagrange equations

Lagrangian mechanics uses generalized coordinates — any independent set of parameters that fully specifies the configuration. For robots, the joint variables (angles for revolute joints, displacements for prismatic) are the natural choice: minimal, complete, and directly tied to actuators.

The Lagrangian is the difference between kinetic and potential energy:

$$L(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}, t) = T(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}) - V(\mathbf{q})$$

Physical systems evolve so as to extremize the time integral of $L$ (the action). Carrying that principle through the calculus of variations gives the Euler-Lagrange equations:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = \tau_i, \quad i = 1, \ldots, n$$

where $\tau_i$ is the generalized force (joint torque/force) for coordinate $i$.

Pick coordinates, write energy, differentiate, substitute — the same five steps work for any robot you will ever meet.

The recipe

1) Express $T$ and $V$ in generalized coordinates. 2) Form $L = T - V$. 3) Take the required partial derivatives. 4) Substitute into the Euler-Lagrange equations. 5) Simplify to the equations of motion.

Why joint variables?

Minimal representation, automatic satisfaction of kinematic constraints, and immediate engineering meaning — each coordinate is an actuator position you can command.

Euler-Lagrange equation (one per DOF)
d/dt( ∂L/∂q̇_i ) − ∂L/∂q_i = τ_i        for i = 1 … n

L = T(q, q̇) − V(q)

Now you assemble the derivation pipeline yourself: drag the five steps into the order that actually produces the equations of motion. Get the sequence wrong and the build stalls — you can't differentiate a Lagrangian you haven't formed yet.

4

Velocity kinematics and the inertia matrix

Correct velocity expressions are the make-or-break step: they set the kinetic energy and therefore the whole model. Velocities are coupled through the chain — the linear velocity of link $i$'s center of mass depends on joints $1$ through $i$.

Using the chain rule,

$$\mathbf{v}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{i} \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_j} \dot{q}_j = \mathbf{J}_{vi}(\mathbf{q}) \dot{\mathbf{q}}$$

For revolute joints, each joint contributes its rate to the angular velocity of every link beyond it (prismatic joints contribute none), so angular velocities add up the chain.

Substituting these velocities back, the kinetic energy becomes a quadratic form in the joint rates:

$$T = \frac{1}{2} \dot{\mathbf{q}}^T \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q}) \dot{\mathbf{q}}$$

where the inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is symmetric and positive definite, with diagonal terms giving the effective inertia at each joint and off-diagonal terms giving inertial coupling between joints.

Once you write $T$ as $\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T\mathbf{D}\dot{\mathbf{q}}$, the entire mass distribution of the robot lives in one configuration-dependent matrix.

Angular velocities add

For a 3-DOF planar arm: $\omega_1 = \dot{q}_1$, $\omega_2 = \dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2$, $\omega_3 = \dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2 + \dot{q}_3$. Each link is driven by an increasing number of joints — that's where coupling terms come from.

Properties of $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$

Always symmetric and positive definite (guaranteeing physical consistency), and it changes with posture — which is exactly why robot dynamics are configuration-dependent.

Inertia matrix entries
D_ij(q) = Σ_k [ m_k (∂r_k/∂q_i)ᵀ (∂r_k/∂q_j)
               + (∂ω_k/∂q_i)ᵀ I_k (∂ω_k/∂q_j) ]

T = ½ q̇ᵀ D(q) q̇

Step a 3-DOF planar arm joint by joint and watch each link inherit the joint rates behind it: $\omega_1 = \dot{\theta}_1$, then $\omega_2 = \dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2$, then $\omega_3 = \dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2+\dot{\theta}_3$. This accumulation is exactly where the off-diagonal coupling in $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is born.

5

Worked walkthrough: Euler-Lagrange on a 2-DOF planar arm

Take a 2-DOF planar arm with revolute joints, link lengths $L_1, L_2$, masses $m_1, m_2$, and inertias $I_1, I_2$. Choose $q_1 = \theta_1$ (first joint) and $q_2 = \theta_2$ (second joint, relative to link 1).

Kinetic energy: link 1 simply rotates about the base with $\dot{\theta}_1$; link 2 has more complex motion because its velocity couples to link 1. $T$ becomes a function of $\theta_1, \theta_2, \dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2$ with cross terms. Potential energy: link 1's height depends on $\theta_1$ only, while link 2's depends on both angles. Apply Euler-Lagrange: the result is a set of coupled, nonlinear ODEs capturing inertial coupling, Coriolis/centrifugal effects, and gravitational loading.

The lesson: even a 2-DOF system produces substantial coupling — which is precisely why we want a systematic, automatable procedure rather than ad-hoc force balances.

Two joints already give you nonlinear, coupled equations — imagine doing this with free-body diagrams for six.

What the equations contain

Inertial coupling between the joints, velocity-dependent Coriolis and centrifugal terms, and configuration-dependent gravity loading — all falling out of one scalar Lagrangian.

Now you build the 2-DOF arm's Lagrangian by choosing the right energy pieces from a tray that includes deliberate traps. Add the kinetic terms (including the $\cos\theta_2$ coupling) and subtract the two gravity terms — drop a sign or grab $\theta_2$ alone instead of $\theta_1+\theta_2$ and the build flags it.

6

From the Lagrangian to the standard dynamic equation

Carrying the Euler-Lagrange machinery through $T = \frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ yields, term by term, the standard robot dynamic equation:

$$\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$$

The derivation naturally separates into three physically meaningful pieces. The inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ comes straight from kinetic energy (symmetric, positive definite). The Coriolis/centrifugal term $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ arises from time derivatives of $\mathbf{D}$ and is quadratic in the joint rates. The gravity term $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$, with $c_i = \partial V/\partial q_i$, comes straight from potential energy.

Every symbol earns its place: $\mathbf{D}$ is mass, $\mathbf{h}$ is the price of moving, $\mathbf{c}$ is the pull of gravity.

$\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ — inertia

From kinetic energy. Effective inertia and coupling between joints. Symmetric, positive definite, configuration-dependent.

$\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ — Coriolis/centrifugal

From time derivatives of $\mathbf{D}$. Velocity-dependent: $h_i = \sum_j \sum_k h_{ijk}\,\dot{q}_j\dot{q}_k$, with $h_{ijk} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\partial D_{ij}/\partial q_k + \partial D_{ik}/\partial q_j - \partial D_{jk}/\partial q_i)$.

$\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — gravity

From potential energy. Conservative gravitational loading: $c_i = \partial V/\partial q_i$.

Standard robot dynamic equation
D(q) q̈ + h(q, q̇) + c(q) = τ

Σ_j D_ij q̈_j + h_i(q, q̇) + c_i(q) = τ_i

Now you expand the standard dynamic equation like a grammar: pick a production and watch each symbol unfold into its definition — $\mathbf{D}$ from kinetic energy, $\mathbf{h}$ from derivatives of $\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{c}$ from potential energy. Choose row $i$ and the index notation grows in front of you.

Now you pick a term of $\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{c} = \boldsymbol{\tau}$ and the animated arm shows what it costs: select the inertia term and the arm resists acceleration, select Coriolis/centrifugal and spinning joints fling the tip, select gravity and the arm sags toward the floor.

7

Real-world connection: industrial implementation

The energy-based model is not just theory — it is the backbone of modern industrial robot control. Embedded controllers evaluate $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$, $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$, and $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ at 1000 Hz or faster to do model-based control, trajectory optimization, collision detection, and predictive maintenance.

Robots using full dynamic models routinely achieve sub-0.1 mm accuracy at speed, 20–40% energy savings over simple PID, and 2–3× faster cycle times. In one automotive welding line, moving from PID (±2 mm, 45 s cycle) to dynamic-model control (±0.2 mm, 28 s cycle) delivered 35% faster production, a 90% accuracy improvement, and 25% energy savings.

The same $L = T - V$ you derive by hand is being evaluated a thousand times a second on the factory floor.

Why it pays off

Knowing $\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{h}$, and $\mathbf{c}$ lets the controller pre-compensate for inertia, Coriolis, and gravity instead of reacting after error appears — the source of both the accuracy and the speed gains.

Summary and looking forward

You now have the energy framework end to end: kinetic energy $T = \frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}}$, potential energy $V = \sum m_i \mathbf{g}^T \mathbf{r}_i$, the Lagrangian $L = T - V$, and the Euler-Lagrange route to $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}$.

Energy methods win for robots because they are scalar, eliminate constraint forces automatically, follow one repeatable procedure, give clear physical meaning to each term, and expose structure that enables efficient algorithms. Practice — try these on your own

Small activities to test your grip on today's ideas — a full energy derivation for a 2-link planar arm with point masses at the link tips. Try each one first, then reveal the worked solution to compare.

1 · Kinematics of the two masses

For a 2-link planar arm with all mass concentrated at the link tips, write the Cartesian position of each mass in terms of $L_1, L_2, \theta_1, \theta_2$. Take $\theta_1$ as the first link's angle from the $x$-axis and $\theta_2$ as the second link's angle relative to the first. Draw and label your own schematic before writing anything down.

Self-check after you've tried
  • Mass 1 should depend only on $\theta_1$.
  • Mass 2 should be mass-1's position plus a second-link term using the sum $\theta_1+\theta_2$, not $\theta_2$ alone.
  • Each coordinate should reduce to a fully extended arm along $x$ when $\theta_1=\theta_2=0$.
Show worked solution

Place the base at the origin. The first link reaches mass $m_1$ at its tip:

$$x_1 = L_1\cos\theta_1, \qquad y_1 = L_1\sin\theta_1.$$

The second link starts at mass 1's location and points along the absolute angle $\theta_1+\theta_2$, so mass $m_2$ sits at:

$$x_2 = L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2),$$ $$y_2 = L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2).$$

Check: at $\theta_1=\theta_2=0$ both masses lie on the $x$-axis at $L_1$ and $L_1+L_2$ — the fully extended arm, as expected. $\boxed{(x_1,y_1),\,(x_2,y_2) \text{ as above}}$

2 · Velocities by the chain rule

Differentiate the positions from Task 1 to get the velocity of each mass in terms of the joint rates $\dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2$. Then compute the squared speeds $|\mathbf{v}_1|^2$ and $|\mathbf{v}_2|^2$, since those are what the kinetic energy needs.

Self-check after you've tried
  • $|\mathbf{v}_1|^2$ should be simply $L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2$.
  • $|\mathbf{v}_2|^2$ should contain three groups: an $L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2$ term, an $L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2$ term, and a cross term carrying $\cos\theta_2$.
  • The cross term must vanish at $\theta_2=\pm\pi/2$ (links perpendicular).
Show worked solution

Mass 1. Differentiate:

$$\dot{x}_1 = -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1, \qquad \dot{y}_1 = L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1.$$ $$|\mathbf{v}_1|^2 = \dot{x}_1^2+\dot{y}_1^2 = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2.$$

Mass 2. Let $s_{12}=\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$, $c_{12}=\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$:

$$\dot{x}_2 = -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 - L_2 s_{12}(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2),$$ $$\dot{y}_2 = L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 + L_2 c_{12}(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2).$$

Squaring and adding, the cross terms combine through $\cos\theta_1 c_{12}+\sin\theta_1 s_{12}=\cos\theta_2$:

$$\boxed{|\mathbf{v}_2|^2 = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 + 2L_1 L_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)\cos\theta_2.}$$

Check: at $\theta_2=\pi/2$ the $\cos\theta_2$ term drops out, leaving the two motions decoupled — geometrically the second link's velocity is then orthogonal to the contribution from link 1, as it should be.

3 · Total kinetic energy

Using the squared speeds from Task 2 and the point-mass form $T = \tfrac{1}{2}m_1|\mathbf{v}_1|^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2|\mathbf{v}_2|^2$ (link mass and inertia neglected), write the system's total kinetic energy in terms of $L_1, L_2, m_1, m_2, \theta_2, \dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2$.

Self-check after you've tried
  • Only $\theta_2$ should appear among the angles — $\theta_1$ must drop out, since rigid rotation of the whole arm shouldn't change its speed distribution.
  • $T$ must be quadratic in the joint rates.
  • Setting $m_1=0$ should leave exactly $\tfrac{1}{2}m_2|\mathbf{v}_2|^2$.
Show worked solution

Substitute the squared speeds:

$$T = \tfrac{1}{2}m_1 L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2\Big[L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 + 2L_1 L_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)\cos\theta_2\Big].$$

Grouping by joint rate, this is the quadratic form $T=\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ with

$$D_{11} = (m_1+m_2)L_1^2 + m_2 L_2^2 + 2m_2 L_1 L_2\cos\theta_2,$$ $$D_{12}=D_{21} = m_2 L_2^2 + m_2 L_1 L_2\cos\theta_2, \qquad D_{22} = m_2 L_2^2.$$

Check: $\theta_1$ is absent (rotational invariance of the base), $\mathbf{D}$ is symmetric, and only $\theta_2$ — the relative angle that actually changes the mass geometry — appears. $\boxed{T \text{ as above, } \mathbf{D}(\theta_2) \text{ symmetric}}$

4 · Gravitational potential energy

With gravity acting in the $-y$ direction (magnitude $g$), write the total gravitational potential energy $V$ of the two point masses using the heights from Task 1.

Self-check after you've tried
  • $V$ must depend on position only — no $\dot{\theta}$ terms.
  • It should be linear in $\sin\theta_1$ and $\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$.
  • $V$ should be maximal when the arm points straight up and zero along the horizontal datum.
Show worked solution

Potential energy is $V = m_1 g\,y_1 + m_2 g\,y_2$ with the $y$-coordinates from Task 1:

$$V = m_1 g\,L_1\sin\theta_1 + m_2 g\big[L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\big].$$

Collecting the link-1 terms:

$$\boxed{V = (m_1+m_2)\,g L_1\sin\theta_1 + m_2\,g L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2).}$$

Check: no velocity dependence; with the base as the height datum, $V=0$ when both links lie horizontal ($\theta_1=\theta_1+\theta_2=0$) and is largest when the masses are lifted to the vertical — matching the physical picture that an extended raised arm stores the most gravitational energy.

5 · Assemble the Lagrangian

Combine your results into the Lagrangian $L = T - V$. You don't need to apply the Euler-Lagrange equations yet — just present the single scalar that fully encodes this arm's dynamics, and note which variables it depends on.

Self-check after you've tried
  • $L$ should be one scalar combining the Task-3 kinetic energy minus the Task-4 potential energy.
  • It depends on $\theta_1, \theta_2, \dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2$ (with $\theta_1$ appearing only through gravity).
  • Units of every term should be energy (e.g. $m L^2 \dot{\theta}^2$ and $m g L\sin\theta$).
Show worked solution

Subtract $V$ from $T$:

$$L = \tfrac{1}{2}m_1 L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2\Big[L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 + 2L_1 L_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)\cos\theta_2\Big]$$ $$\quad - (m_1+m_2)g L_1\sin\theta_1 - m_2 g L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2).$$

This one scalar $L(\theta_1,\theta_2,\dot{\theta}_1,\dot{\theta}_2)$ is the complete dynamical description of the arm. Check: the kinetic part is quadratic in the rates (and carries the $\cos\theta_2$ coupling), the potential part is rate-free, and every term has units of energy. Feeding this into $\frac{d}{dt}(\partial L/\partial\dot{\theta}_i)-\partial L/\partial\theta_i=\tau_i$ in the next module reproduces exactly $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})+\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})=\boldsymbol{\tau}$. $\boxed{L=T-V \text{ as above}}$
