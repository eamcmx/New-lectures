The Abacus — a register file made of beads

Humanity's first calculating device (~2500 BCE Mesopotamia, refined into the Chinese suanpan and the Japanese soroban you see below). Guided addition — watch the carry happen

A soroban never "does" 7 + 8 in one motion — there aren't enough beads on one rod. Instead it uses complements: when a rod overflows, add 1 to the next rod and take the ten-complement away here. Notice how… that's exactly a carry propagating through an adder circuit.

Where a bead sits is what it means

The genius here isn't the beads — it's positional notation. The very same bead is worth 1 on the right rod and 1,000,000 on the left one, purely by where it sits. Your CPU inherits the trick unchanged: bit 0 of a word means 1, bit 20 means 1,048,576 — same bit, different column.

The oldest algorithm you know

"No room on this rod → pass 1 to the next rod" is the carry, and it may be the oldest algorithm still in daily use. Soroban complements, the ratcheting gears of Pascal's calculator, and the carry chain inside your ALU are three implementations of one idea, four thousand years apart.

Storage without a processor

An abacus stores state but doesn't process it — every rule ("if it overflows, carry") runs in the operator's head. The human is the control unit; the frame is just registers. Mechanizing that — putting the rules into the machine — was Babbage's leap.
