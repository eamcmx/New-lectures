Honest orders of magnitude: a practised abacus user moves about one bead per second; the Mark I managed ~3 additions; ENIAC hit ~5,000 operations — its famous 1000× jump; the 4004 ran ~10⁵ instructions; a modern multicore CPU sustains ~10¹¹. Quantum machines don't join this race at all — a qubit's superposition trades raw ops/s for a different kind of parallelism.

Every era solved the last era's bottleneck

Human speed capped the abacus and slide rule, so Babbage automated. Gears and relays wore, stuck and drifted, so the 1940s went electronic for reliability. Tubes ran hot, huge and costly, so transistors and ICs shrank size and cost. And when shrinking, ever-faster cores hit the heat wall, we went parallel. Notice how each era's headline feature is exactly the previous era's headache.

Programmability was the true revolution

Jacquard's punched cards and Babbage's Analytical Engine did something subtler than speed: they separated the machine from the task. One loom, any pattern; one Mill, any formula. Every device before them was built for a single job — everything after is one machine wearing endless costumes. Ada Lovelace saw it first: a symbol machine need not stop at numbers.

Architecture outlives organization

The 1945 Von Neumann blueprint — one memory holding both data and instructions, marched through a fetch–decode–execute cycle — still describes your laptop, your phone, and the server behind this page. How machines are built (tubes → transistors → billions of FinFETs) changes every decade; what they do has held for 80 years. See it run in the Von Neumann simulator.
