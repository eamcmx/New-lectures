The Analytical Engine

Programming a computer made of brass. Charles Babbage designed a general-purpose, steam-powered computer in 1837 — a century early, and never built. Punch a chain of cards below, set the engine going, and watch the Mill turn figures in the Store. Load a preset, then press ▶ Run — or Step through card by card (Space steps while paused).

The machine

1837 · Charles Babbage · brass & steam

Designed from 1837, refined for decades, never fully built. A steam-powered general-purpose engine with two parts: the Mill (the arithmetic unit) and the Store — a memory of about 1,000 columns, each a stack of 40 decimal figure wheels. The whole design would have filled a room.

How it was programmed

Chains of punched cards, borrowed from the Jacquard loom: operation cards armed the Mill (+ − × ÷), variable cards steered values between Store and Mill, number cards supplied constants. Backing barrels could rewind the chain when the run-up lever tripped — loops and conditional branching, decades before electricity.

What it was for

Error-free mathematical tables — and more. In 1843 Ada Lovelace published “Note G”: a card program to compute Bernoulli numbers, celebrated as the first computer program. She saw further still — an engine that weaves symbols could act on music, algebra, anything with rules; not just numbers.

Example programs

Babbage’s demo — tabulate x² + x + 41

Why this mattered: Victorian Britain ran on printed mathematical tables — navigation, banking, engineering — computed by human “computers” and riddled with errors; mechanizing them away was the engines’ whole purpose. By finite differences a quadratic needs no multiplication at all: the second difference is the constant 2, so the loop is pure addition. Euler’s polynomial x²+x+41 was a favorite showpiece — every value printed here is prime.

Lovelace’s spirit — a recurrence (n!)

Why this mattered: Note G computed Bernoulli numbers via a much longer chain of these very same card moves — this preset uses her exact techniques (a loop of backing cards, working variables, a counter) on a shorter recurrence: factorials up to 7! = 5040. Lovelace — the first programmer — also saw past arithmetic: a machine that follows rules over symbols could just as well weave music or algebra.

Conditional power — |a − b|

Why this mattered: subtract; if the run-up lever trips (the result ran negative), the chain backs up, the operands swap, and the subtraction runs again. No earlier machine could change its own course based on a computed result — this one card is the ancestor of every if, while, and conditional jump.

Simplifications — where we cheat

  • The real Store held ~1,000 columns of 40 decimal digits; we show 12 columns of 6 digits plus a sign.
  • Babbage used three separate card streams (operation, variable, number) fed through separate readers; we thread one single chain.
  • CB/CB? compress the real mechanism — backing and advancing barrels driven by the run-up lever — into one card.
  • Our run-up lever stays tripped until a CB? card consults it; the real levers were reset mechanically as the apparatus cycled.
  • Timing: the design managed roughly one addition per second, and a large multiplication took minutes; here it is a slider.
  • Stepped multiplication/division, the anticipating carriage, and the printing & stereotyping apparatus are all abstracted to single moves. OP / yields the integer quotient.

Mill + Store = CPU + memory

Babbage split his engine into a Mill that computes and a Store that remembers — exactly the split von Neumann formalized in 1945, and the one inside every chip you own. Notice how every card either moves a value Store↔Mill or turns the Mill: so does every load/store instruction today. Go further: try the full Von Neumann simulator.

The run-up lever is a status flag

When a result runs negative or overflows, a physical lever trips — and a combinatorial card consults it to rewind the chain. Your CPU does the same: the ALU sets negative/overflow/carry flags and a conditional jump reads them. Branching on a computed result is what separates a computer from a calculator.

Lovelace’s leap: it’s all symbols

Lovelace saw that the cards don’t care that the wheels hold digits — the Engine “weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves,” and could act on music or any symbols governed by rules. That is the founding insight of computer science: computation is symbol manipulation; numbers are just one costume.
