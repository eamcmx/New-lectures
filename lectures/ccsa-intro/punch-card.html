Punch Cards — programs made of holes

Before electronics, before even Babbage, a program was a physical object: a chain of cards with holes in it. Click or drag on the card chain below to punch and fill holes, and watch the fabric answer. Then jump to 1890 and feed a census deck through Hollerith's electric tabulator.

The story in three holes

1801 · Jacquard's loom

Joseph-Marie Jacquard controls a silk loom with a chain of punched cards. Each card is one row of the pattern; its holes decide which warp threads lift. Binary instructions, stored mechanically.

1890 · Hollerith's tabulator

Herman Hollerith reads punch cards electrically for the US Census: spring-loaded pins probe the card, and where a pin finds a hole it closes a circuit and a counter ticks. The census that was going to take a decade takes about two years.

1924 · a company renamed

Hollerith's Tabulating Machine Company merges and is renamed the International Business Machines Corporation — IBM. Punched cards remain the standard way to feed computers into the 1970s.

Click the holes — all three views are the same card from the loom above. Hole or no hole, 1 or 0, opcode bit or operand bit: the physical medium changed from cardboard to charge, but an instruction is still just a row of yes/no marks with an agreed meaning.

Machine ≠ pattern

Jacquard's real leap was separating the machine from the pattern. One loom, infinite fabrics — just swap the card chain. Replace “loom” with CPU and “card chain” with program and you have the core idea of general-purpose computing: one CPU, infinite programs.

One-hot never died

Hollerith's one hole per field is one-hot encoding, and it is still how your CPU works: the decoder turns a dense opcode into one-hot control signals — exactly one line per action goes high, and that line “closes the circuit” that enables the ALU, a register write, a memory read.

Born commercial

Data processing wasn't born in a lab — it was born in the 1890 census, a government counting problem with a deadline. Hollerith rented machines, sold cards, and his company became IBM in 1924. The business of computing is older than the electronic computer itself.
