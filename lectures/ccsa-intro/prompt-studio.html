Why prompts matter the PAAL idea

The goal of using an AI in this course is not answers — it is calibrated practice: questions pitched just above your level, with your reasoning checked step by step. A good prompt turns the AI into a coach that quizzes you on the machines and waits for you to commit; a lazy prompt turns it into a homework printer that quietly does the learning for you. And one extra warning for this lecture: history is a hallucination hotspot — models confidently produce wrong dates and merge machines together — so every factual claim gets verified against your notes.

🟢 AI as coach Quiz me on the machines, check my Turing-machine trace, probe my architecture-vs-organization reasoning. Commit-before-reveal, one question at a time. This is where learning happens.
🟡 AI as reference Dates, tube counts, specs. Usable — but models hallucinate history constantly: wrong years, merged machines, invented "firsts". Verify every claim against the lecture before you trust it.
🔴 Avoid: AI as answer machine Pasting the assignment and copying the output = skill atrophy. You will meet the exam alone, and it does not accept screenshots of a chat.

The verification habit non-negotiable — especially for history

  • ✓Dates and "firsts" are the classic AI failure. Cross-check every "first computer" claim against the lecture's careful distinctions: first electronic digital device (ABC, 1939, not programmable) ≠ first working programmable computer (Z3, 1941) ≠ first general-purpose all-electronic (ENIAC, 1945) ≠ first stored-program design (von Neumann's 1945 EDVAC report). An AI that hands you one flat "first" has already failed the question.
  • ✓Watch for merged machines. Models love blending details into one franken-computer — Mark I's paper tape on ENIAC, ENIAC's tube count on the Z3's relays. If a paragraph describes a machine, check each spec belongs to that machine.
  • ✓Ask the AI to argue the opposite. "Now convince me the Z3 was not the first programmable computer." Whatever survives the counter-argument is what you actually understand — and fabricated facts rarely survive their own rebuttal.
  • ✓Numbers are checkable — check them. If it says Moore's law takes the Intel 4004's 2,300 transistors to ~150 billion after 26 doublings, don't nod along: 2,300 × 2²⁶ is arithmetic you can do yourself (2¹⁰ ≈ 10³ makes it a ten-second estimate).
  • ✓Cross-check with the interactives in this set: walk the grand timeline, run the Z3 and ENIAC pages, step the stored-program machine, plot the Moore's-law curve. Pixels don't hallucinate.

Protocol beats content

Notice how every strong prompt here specifies an interaction protocol — one question at a time, commit before reveal, first-wrong-step only. That single rule is what turns a chat into practice: it forces retrieval, and retrieval is where memory forms.

Fluency ≠ truth

An AI paragraph putting ENIAC in 1943 with 18,000 relays reads exactly as smoothly as the correct one (1945, 17,468 vacuum tubes). In history, confident wrong dates abound — which is why the verification clause is baked into every prompt this studio builds, and why the interactive pages are your ground truth.

Raise difficulty, not comfort

The best students use AI to increase difficulty — "make the anachronisms subtler", "make me defend the other side", "two right in a row means harder" — not to decrease effort. The struggle you're tempted to outsource is the product.
