The Atanasoff–Berry Computer

The first electronic digital computer (1939–42) — 300 vacuum tubes, binary numbers spinning on capacitor drums, and exactly one talent: solving systems of linear equations. It had no program. You were the control unit. Below, take the operator's chair: load equations onto the drums, fire the add–subtract mechanism, punch the results to cards, and feel why programmability was the missing piece.

The Machine

1939–1942 · Iowa State

Designed by physics professor John Atanasoff and graduate student Clifford Berry. First electronic digital computing device: 300+ vacuum tubes doing binary arithmetic and logic. Numbers lived as 50-bit binary words on two rotating capacitor drums — regenerative memory, refreshed every turn (the ancestor of DRAM refresh).

How it was operated

Not programmable, not general-purpose. There was no instruction store at all. A human operator fed decimal cards in, threw switches to pick the coefficient to kill, watched the drums turn at 60 rpm, and punched each intermediate result onto a binary card — then fed those cards back in. The operator was the control unit, executing Gaussian elimination step by step, for hours.

What it solved

One job: systems of up to 29 simultaneous linear equations. Atanasoff needed this for physics — big linear systems fall out of fitting spectra data and approximating PDEs in quantum mechanics and structural problems. By hand, 29 equations took weeks; the ABC did the arithmetic electronically, flawlessly.

Simplifications — where we cheat

  • Size: the real ABC handled up to 29 equations in 29 unknowns; we shrink to 2–3 so you can see every bit.
  • Word length: real drums held 50-bit base-2 fixed-point words (≈15 decimal digits); our binary view shows 16-bit (8.8) words.
  • Parallel hardware: the machine had ~30 add–subtract mechanisms working in parallel — one per coefficient — so a whole equation was combined on every drum revolution. We animate one sweeping pass for clarity.
  • One click = one elimination: on the real machine, zeroing a single coefficient took many drum revolutions of nonrestoring shift-and-subtract; we compress that into one animated firing.
  • Timing: the drums really turned at 60 rpm, but the operator's card punching and re-feeding dominated — hours of it for a large system. Our "operator time" counter estimates that honest cost.
  • The real intermediate cards were written by burning spots with high voltage (and misread about 1 time in 100,000 — the bug that kept the ABC from ever finishing a 29×29 system!). Our cards never misread.

Regenerative drums → DRAM

The drum capacitors leaked charge, so every revolution the machine read each bit and wrote it right back — refresh. Notice how that is exactly what the DRAM in your laptop does thousands of times a second, in 2025 as in 1939. Cheap, dense, forgetful memory plus a refresh cycle beat expensive memory that remembers on its own.

Parallel before programmable

The ABC had ~30 add–subtract mechanisms firing together — one per coefficient — so each drum turn combined an entire equation at once. That is SIMD avant la lettre: one operation, many data lanes, the same idea as your CPU's vector units and your GPU. Parallelism showed up in hardware before the stored program did.

The missing piece was control

Did you feel it? The arithmetic was instant — it was you shuttling cards, choosing coefficients, sequencing steps. The ABC proved electronic arithmetic; what it lacked was a control unit. The Z3, Mark I and finally the stored-program machine put the operator on tape — and then into memory itself.
