The Slide Rule — multiplication you can slide

Drag the middle slide left and right, and drag the glass cursor with the red hairline. Invented in the 1620s — barely a decade after logarithms themselves — the slide rule lived in every engineer's pocket until the 1970s, and rode along through the Mercury, Gemini and Apollo programs. It multiplies by adding distances: each scale is ruled so that distance = log of the number, and log a + log b = log ab.

Analog trades exactness for speed and simplicity; digital trades circuitry for exactness. Three good figures, instantly, with zero electricity — that bargain held for 350 years.

Logarithms turn × into +

Napier's theorem — log ab = log a + log b — is usually a line in a textbook. The slide rule is that theorem made physical: two sticks ruled in logarithms, so that laying distances end-to-end adds logs, and adding logs multiplies numbers. Notice how the ticks crowd together toward 10 — that crowding is the logarithm.

It's an analog computer

The answer is a position, not a symbol — the scales encode the math the way op-amp circuits embody equations, and reading the hairline is like reading a voltage. Your eye is the analog-to-digital converter, the same job the astrolabe asked of its user. No clock, no steps, no program: the relationship is built into the hardware, and the answer simply is where it is.

To the Moon on three figures

Engineers carried slide rules through Mercury, Gemini and Apollo — a Pickett N600-ES flew on Apollo 13. Digital computers won in the 1970s not because they were more elegant, but on accuracy and automation: ten digits instead of three, and no human needed to track the decimal point. Elegance lost to exactness — and that is most of the story of this course.
