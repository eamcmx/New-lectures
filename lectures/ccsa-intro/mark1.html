The Harvard Mark I — fifty feet of clockwork arithmetic

A room-sized electromechanical calculator that read its program from a punched paper tape, one instruction per line, and clicked through it at three additions per second — for fifteen years straight. Load a preset below, press ▶ Run, and watch the digit wheels turn. Notice the machine-time readout: you are about to feel how slow "fast" once was.

The Machine

1944 · Aiken & IBM · relays and shafts

The IBM Automatic Sequence Controlled Calculator (ASCC), completed in 1944 and installed at Harvard. Roughly 750,000 electromechanical components — relays, rotating shafts, clutches — synchronized by a 50-foot drive shaft turned by a 5-horsepower motor. A decimal machine: 72 accumulator registers of 23 digits plus sign. Speed: ~3 additions/second; a multiplication took ~6 seconds. It ran nearly continuously for ~15 years.

How it was programmed

A 24-channel punched paper tape, one instruction per line, fed through a reader: each line said take from this unit, deliver to that unit, do this. There was no conditional branch — long computations meant tapes many meters long, and a loop meant physically splicing the tape into a loop. Grace Hopper was one of its first programmers (and later taped the legendary "first actual bug" — a moth — into the logbook of its successor, Mark II).

What it actually computed

Mathematical tables. It ground out Bessel function tables for years — the staff nicknamed it "Bessie". It computed Navy gunnery and ballistics tables, and in 1944 John von Neumann ran implosion calculations for the Manhattan Project on it. Table-making, one difference at a time: the defining computational job of the era.

1946: crude branching arrives. Two years in, a "subsidiary sequence unit" was bolted on, letting the machine pick between a few auxiliary tapes — the first whiff of a conditional branch. But for years before that, Bessie just looped: if you wanted n iterations, you glued the tape's ends together and let the reader go around. The if-statement you take for granted was once a hardware upgrade.

Simplifications — where this simulator cheats

  • 8 accumulators, not 72 — and 8 digits each, not 23 digits + sign.
  • 4 constant registers, not 60 (the real ones were set by 1,440 rotary switches).
  • Friendly assembler, not hole codes — the real tape carried numeric unit codes punched across 24 channels (OUT | IN | MISC); our hole patterns are suggestive, not the true encoding.
  • Timing compressed — the multiply–divide unit is slowed only 4–6× on screen; the "real machine time" readout uses the true speeds instead (add 0.3 s, multiply 6 s, divide 15 s, typing a result ≈ 5 s).
  • The loop toggle stands in for glue — real loops were paper, scissors, and splice cement.

Receiving means adding

Notice how a plain transfer A3 -> A5 doesn't replace A5 — it adds into it. That's not a quirk, it's the machine's nature: an accumulator is a counter wheel, and delivering a number turns the wheel. This is the direct ancestor of the += operator and of the accumulate path inside every modern ALU. When you had to overwrite, you cleared first — count how many CLEAR lines the presets need.

The cost model was visible

Multiplication cost ~20 additions; division cost ~50. You could hear the difference. So algorithm design meant counting operations — which is exactly why preset ① computes squares with two additions instead of one multiplication. Nothing has changed but the units: today we count FLOPs versus memory traffic, and the best algorithms still route around the expensive unit.

Harvard architecture — this page's namesake

The program lived on paper tape; the data lived in accumulator wheels. Two physically separate paths — instructions could never be data. Von Neumann's shared-memory design won the war of ideas, but the Harvard idea never died: your CPU's split L1 instruction / L1 data caches (L1i / L1d) are exactly this tape-versus-wheels separation, rebuilt in silicon for speed.
