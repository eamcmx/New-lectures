Moore's Law — the exponential that built the modern world

In 1965 Gordon Moore noticed that the number of components engineers could squeeze onto a chip was doubling every year (revised in 1975 to every two years). Before the curve: five generations of switch

Moore's Law only became possible because the switch itself kept being reinvented. Each step below fixed the failure mode of the one before it. The transistor arrived in 1947 at Bell Labs (Bardeen, Brattain & Shockley); the integrated circuit in 1958–59 (Kilby at TI, Noyce at Fairchild) — and from there, the doubling began.

Electromechanical relay

Zuse Z3, 1941 · ~10 Hz · centimetre-scale

A literal metal arm clacking open and shut. Reliable-ish, but glacial — physics limits a moving part to a few switches per second.Failure mode: too slow, wears out.

→

Vacuum tube

ENIAC, 1945 · ~100 kHz switching · thumb-sized, hot

No moving parts — 10,000× faster. But a tube burns out like the light bulb it resembles: ENIAC ran 17,468 of them, and technicians replaced failed tubes daily.Failure mode: burns out, guzzles power.

→

Transistor

Bell Labs 1947 · TRADIC 1954 · millimetre-scale, cool

Solid state: nothing to burn out. Faster, smaller, cheaper, reliable. TRADIC (1954), the first transistorized computer, used just 800 transistors vs ENIAC's 17,468 tubes.Failure mode: wiring thousands by hand.

→

Integrated circuit

Kilby & Noyce, 1958–59 · many switches per chip

Print the transistors and their wiring onto one sliver of silicon. Now shrinking the process shrinks everything at once — the ratchet Moore observed in 1965.Failure mode: none fatal — just "how many can we fit?"

→
fins ≈ 5 nm wide

Modern FinFET

~5 nm class · billions per chip

The transistor went 3-D: a fin of silicon wrapped by its gate, thousands of times thinner than a hair. 134 billion of these fit in an M2 Ultra.Failure mode: atoms are only so small…

This ratchet is what powered the microprocessor (Intel 4004, 1971) and then the PC era — Altair 8800 (1975), Apple II (1977) — each generation cheaper and faster than the last.

The plateau: clock speed hit a wall in ~2004

Transistor counts kept doubling right through this chart — but around 2004, clock frequency slammed into the power wall: switching billions of transistors at ever-higher GHz produced more heat per mm² than could be pulled out of the chip. The grey dashed line is the trend that didn't happen. The free lunch ended, and the industry's answer was multicore parallelism: spend the still-doubling transistors on more cores side by side instead of one faster core. It was never physics

Moore's Law is not a law of nature — it's an observation that became a promise. Fabs, toolmakers, and chip designers all planned their roadmaps around the next doubling, so the doubling happened: a self-fulfilling economic contract the whole industry signed. When you plan around an exponential, you build one.

Exponentials break intuition

Notice how the linear chart hid fifty years of history on the floor — 26 doublings turned a calculator chip into a data center, and your eyes couldn't see 25 of them. Before trusting any tech chart, ask: linear or log? A "hockey stick" and a straight log line are the same curve wearing different axes.

Architecture is what you do when physics says no

When one exponential stalled (clock speed, 2004), another took over: multicore CPUs, then GPUs, then dedicated accelerators. Same transistor budget, spent smarter. This is why the rest of this course is about architecture — the art of getting more computing out of the switches physics gives you.
