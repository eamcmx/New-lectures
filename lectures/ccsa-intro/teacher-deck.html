S1 · Definitions

What is computer architecture?

The design, organization and implementation of a computer system — the blueprint the programmer can see.

ISA — instructions, opcodes, addressing modes, registers Memory — hierarchy, caches, virtual memory I/O — how data gets in and out Parallelism — pipelining, superscalar, multiprocessing Buses — communication protocols
Stress the word "blueprint": an architect of a building decides where the doors and rooms are, not which brand of brick. Ask students what they think a programmer can actually "see" of the hardware — most will guess RAM size or clock speed, which tees up the architecture-vs-organization distinction two slides from now. The five chips are the syllabus map for the whole term; each becomes its own week later.
S1 · Definitions

Why study it?

  • Write efficient software — code that fits how the machine actually works
  • Design better hardware — know which choices cost what
  • Benchmark systems — compare machines on evidence, not marketing
  • Understand what's coming — RISC → multicore → neuromorphic → quantum
A good anecdote here: two loops over the same array, one row-major and one column-major, can differ 10x in speed purely because of caches — the language never tells you that, the architecture does. Ask who in the room has wondered why their phone chip is "ARM" and their laptop is "x86"; promise the answer arrives in the modern-era section. This slide is the motivation contract for the course.
S1 · The exam distinction

Architecture vs Organization

Architecture

  • What the system does
  • Programmer-visible: ISA, registers, memory model
  • Fixed early — shapes all software
  • Optimizes performance & capability

Organization

  • How it's physically built
  • Circuits, gates, RTL, microarchitecture
  • Changes with IC technology
  • Optimizes cost, power, size, reliability

Architecture buys capability. Organization buys efficiency.

This is THE exam distinction — flag it explicitly and slow down. Use the multiplication example: whether an ISA has a multiply instruction is architecture; whether that multiply is a dedicated circuit or repeated addition underneath is organization. Point out that every x86 chip from 1978 to today runs largely the same architecture while the organization has been rebuilt dozens of times — that is exactly why old software still runs.
S1 · Ask the room

Is cache size architecture…
or organization?

🖐️ Take a vote.

Trick question — capacity visible to the ISA is architecture; a pure implementation choice invisible to software is organization. The border is exactly where programmer visibility ends.

Actually run the vote — hands up for each side — before saying anything. Whichever side wins, play devil's advocate: cache size is usually a transparent organizational choice, yet the moment the ISA exposes it (cache-control instructions, documented capacities software depends on) it hardens into architecture. The takeaway to say out loud: the line is not a property of the component, it is a property of what the programmer is promised.
S2 · Early mechanical devices

The abacus c. 2500 BCE, Mesopotamia

  • Beads = register values
  • Columns = orders of magnitude

A register file you flick with your fingers — the operator is the ALU.

🔬 Live demo abacus.html
Open the demo and add two numbers live — deliberately make a carry mistake and let the class catch it, because the carry IS the algorithm. Point out that the abacus stores state but does not compute: every operation lives in the trained fingers of the operator. That split — dumb storage plus a smart agent — is the theme every machine in this lecture tries to erase.
S2 · Early mechanical devices

Antikythera mechanism c. 100 BCE, Greece

  • 30+ bronze gears — a geared analog computer
  • Predicted eclipses and Olympiad dates

Each gear ratio is a hard-wired function — the geartrain is mechanical software.

🔬 Live demo timeline.html — find it on the timeline
Tell the shipwreck story: sponge divers pulled a corroded lump out of the Aegean in 1901, and it took a century plus X-ray tomography to reveal 30-plus precision gears — nothing that sophisticated appears again for 1,400 years. Ask the room what "programming" this machine would mean; the answer is cutting new gears, which makes the point that here the hardware IS the program. Open the timeline demo and let them see how alone it sits in its era.
S2 · Early mechanical devices

The astrolabe c. 200 BCE

  • Sight a star along the alidade, read its angle
  • Rotating plates turn the angle into time, latitude, star positions

An analog-to-digital converter you hold up to the sky.

🔬 Live demo timeline.html — see it in context
The concept to land is measurement-as-input: the astrolabe takes a continuous physical quantity (a star's altitude) and converts it into usable numbers — the ancestor of every sensor and ADC in the room's phones. Mention it was the GPS of the medieval world for both navigation and prayer times, refined for centuries in the Islamic world. Quick question for the room: what does your phone use instead of an alidade? (Accelerometers, GPS receivers — same job, new physics.)
S2 · Early mechanical devices

The slide rule 1600s – 1970s

  • Logarithmic scales: multiplication becomes addition of lengths
  • Analog computation — answers by alignment, not counting
  • Flew on the Apollo program

Three centuries of engineering ran on a stick.

🔬 Live demo slide-rule.html
Do a live multiplication in the demo — 2 × 3 — and show the answer is found, not calculated: log(a)+log(b) as physical distance. The Apollo detail always lands: engineers carried slide rules as backup while flying to the Moon, and Buzz Aldrin had one aboard. Preempt the misconception that analog means primitive — analog computing is currently returning in neuromorphic chips, so this slide is secretly about the future too.
S3 · The mechanical era

Babbage's Difference Engine 1822

  • Steam-powered polynomial tabulator
  • Method of finite differences — only additions, cranked repeatedly
  • Goal: error-free mathematical tables

A fixed-function accelerator: one algorithm, cast in iron.

Motivate with the problem: Victorian navigation and engineering ran on printed log tables full of human copying errors, and errors sank ships — Babbage's line was "I wish to God these calculations had been executed by steam." Explain finite differences in one breath: any polynomial can be tabulated with nothing but repeated additions, which is why a crank sufficed. Note the machine was never finished in his lifetime — but the London Science Museum built one in 1991 from his plans and it works perfectly.
S3 · The mechanical era

The Analytical Engine 1837

  • First general-purpose programmable design
  • Mill = CPU  ·  Store = memory  ·  cards = program
  • Loops and conditional branching — on paper, in 1837

A full computer, designed a century early — in brass.

🔬 Live demo analytical-engine.html
The jump from the Difference Engine matters more than either machine: Babbage went from "a machine for one calculation" to "a machine for ANY calculation" — that conceptual leap is the birth of the computer. Map Mill/Store/cards onto CPU/memory/program explicitly and tell them this vocabulary comes back on the von Neumann slide. Run the demo and step a short program so they see loops and branching in brass.
S3 · The mechanical era

Ada Lovelace 1843, Note G

  • Published the first algorithm for the Analytical Engine (Bernoulli numbers)
  • The first programmer — for a machine that was never built
  • Saw computing beyond numbers: symbols, music, anything encodable

"The Engine might compose elaborate pieces of music…" — a century before software.

The astonishing part to emphasize: she wrote and debugged a program for a machine that existed only on paper — pure reasoning about an architecture, which is exactly the skill this course teaches. Her deeper insight in Note G is that the Engine manipulates symbols, not just quantities: if music can be encoded, the Engine could compose. Ask the room what she was describing — they will realize it is software, general-purpose computing, and arguably AI speculation, in 1843.
S3 · The mechanical era

The Jacquard loom 1801

  • Punched cards control every thread of the weave
  • Hole / no-hole = binary instruction storage
  • Change the cards → change the pattern — no rebuilding

The program is the cards. Babbage was watching.

🔬 Live demo punch-card.html
Note the date — this is BEFORE Babbage, and he explicitly borrowed the card idea; he even owned a woven portrait of Jacquard made from 24,000 cards. The loom separates the machine from the task: one loom, infinite patterns, which is the essence of programmability. In the demo, punch a few holes and show the pattern change — hole/no-hole is the same 1/0 the room's laptops are running right now.
S3 · The mechanical era

Hollerith's tabulator 1890 US Census

  • Punched cards read electrically — pins through holes close circuits
  • Census done in years, not a decade
  • His company became IBM 1924

Data processing becomes an industry — before computing does.

🔬 Live demo punch-card.html
Set up the crisis: the 1880 census took about eight years to tabulate by hand, and the 1890 one was projected to overrun the decade — the state literally could not count its own people. Hollerith's twist on Jacquard is electrical reading: a pin through a hole closes a circuit, marrying punch cards to electricity for the first time. Punchline for the room: the "machines" in International Business Machines meant tabulators — IBM is older than the computer it became famous for.
S4 · Birth of digital

Turing, On Computable Numbers 1936

  • Tape + head + state table = a machine that computes anything computable
  • Defined computability — and its limits — before any computer existed
  • The universal machine: one machine simulating every other

The idea of the computer, proved as a theorem.

🔬 Live demo turing-machine.html
Frame the irony: the most important computer of the 1930s was imaginary — Turing built it out of mathematics to answer Hilbert's decision problem, and the computer fell out as a by-product. The universal machine idea deserves a full stop: ONE machine that, fed a description of any other machine, becomes it — that is why your laptop can be a typewriter, a synthesizer and a flight simulator. Step the demo machine a few transitions live; students are always surprised how little machinery "universal" requires.
S4 · Birth of digital

Atanasoff–Berry Computer 1939

  • First electronic digital computing device — 300+ vacuum tubes
  • Solved systems of linear equations
  • Not programmable — one job, wired in

Electronic: yes. Digital: yes. A computer as we mean it: not yet.

🔬 Live demo abc.html
Use this slide to sharpen definitions: ask the room whether the ABC is "a computer," and let them argue — it is electronic and digital but not programmable, so the answer depends on which property you think is essential. Worth mentioning: a 1973 court case (Honeywell v. Sperry Rand) invalidated the ENIAC patent partly by crediting Atanasoff, so even the law had to decide what "first computer" means. The demo shows its one trick — Gaussian elimination in tubes and capacitors.
S4 · Birth of digital

Zuse's Z3 1941, Berlin

  • First working programmable automatic digital computer
  • 2,400 relays · binary floating point · 5–10 Hz
  • Program on punched film — but no conditional branch

Built in a Berlin flat, from telephone parts, ignored by everyone.

🔬 Live demo z3.html
The human story sells it: Zuse built the Z3 in his parents' living room from salvaged telephone relays, the German government saw no war value in it, and the original was destroyed in a 1943 air raid. Technically he was ahead of everyone — binary and floating point in 1941, choices we still use — at a majestic 5 to 10 Hz. Ask why "no conditional branch" matters: without it a program cannot make decisions, so the Z3 runs recipes, not algorithms with choices (though it was later proven Turing-complete via a clever hack).
S4 · Birth of digital

Harvard Mark I 1944

  • IBM-built, 750,000 parts, 15 m long — electromechanical
  • Program on 24-channel paper tape
  • Computed tables for the US Navy — crew called her "Bessie"

Instructions on tape, data in counters — "Harvard architecture" still means separate paths.

🔬 Live demo mark1.html
Play the sound in their heads: 750,000 parts clacking in sync, described as "a roomful of ladies knitting" — computation you could hear. This machine is why we say "Harvard architecture" today: program on tape, data in counters, two separate paths — connect it forward to microcontrollers and CPU caches that still split instructions from data. Grace Hopper programmed Bessie, and the famous logbook "bug" — an actual moth taped into the log — comes from this lineage.
S4 · Birth of digital

ENIAC 1945

  • First general-purpose all-electronic computer
  • 17,468 tubes · 30 tons · a room-sized machine
  • 5,000 additions/s — ~1000× faster than anything before

The moment computing left mechanical time and entered electronic time.

🔬 Live demo eniac.html
Give the scale a body: 30 tons, 17,468 tubes, and the (exaggerated but tellable) legend that Philadelphia's lights dimmed when it switched on. The real headline is the factor of ~1000 — mechanical relays click at tens of hertz, tubes switch in microseconds, and no single jump in computing history is bigger. Preempt the "tubes always burning out" myth: run gently, ENIAC often went days between failures, which for 17,468 hot glass bottles is astonishing engineering.
S4 · Birth of digital

The six who programmed it

  • Six women — McNulty, Jennings, Snyder, Wescoff, Bilas, Lichterman
  • "Programming" = rewiring plugboards and setting thousands of switches
  • Mission: artillery firing tables — a trajectory computed faster than the shell flies

The hardware got the headlines. The software was invented at the plugboard.

Tell it straight: the six were hired as human "computers" calculating trajectories by hand, then handed wiring diagrams and told to make ENIAC do it — no manuals, no training, they reverse-engineered the machine and invented debugging, subroutine thinking and program planning on the way. At the 1946 press unveiling they went largely uncredited; some were assumed to be models posing with the machine. Ask the room what "programming" even means when there is no stored program — the answer (physically reshaping the machine) sets up the next slide perfectly.
S4 · Birth of digital

The stored-program concept 1945, EDVAC report

  • Data and instructions in one memory
  • CPU (ALU + registers + control) · memory · I/O · a system bus
  • Programs become data: loadable, editable, self-modifying

Turing's universal machine, realized in hardware — the blueprint we still use.

🔬 Live demo stored-program.html 🖥️ CPU simulator sim/vnmsim.html
This is the hinge of the entire lecture — say so. Contrast with the previous slide: reprogramming ENIAC meant days of rewiring, whereas a stored program is just different bytes in memory — the machine reshapes itself in microseconds. Emphasize the philosophical jolt that instructions ARE data (that is what makes compilers, operating systems and loading an app from disk possible), then run stored-program.html and show an instruction being modified in memory. Credit note: the ideas drew heavily on Eckert and Mauchly, but the report carried von Neumann's name alone.

The arc

→
ElectronicsABC, tubes
→
ProgrammabilityZ3, ENIAC
→
Stored programvon Neumann

Each step asks: what should the machine do — and what should stay flexible?

Pause here and let the class breathe — this one slide is the whole first half of the lecture. Walk the four steps and ask which single step they would call "the invention of the computer"; there is no right answer, and the argument itself teaches the definitions. Point out that the question at the bottom — what is fixed versus what stays flexible — is literally the architecture-versus-organization question from slide 4, now visible across 4,000 years.
S5 · Evolution

The transistor 1947, Bell Labs

  • Bardeen, Brattain, Shockley — Nobel Prize 1956
  • No heat, no glass, no warm-up: a solid-state switch
  • TRADIC 1954 — first transistorized computer, 800 transistors

The tube dies; the switch shrinks. And shrinks. And shrinks.

Compare the switch physically: a vacuum tube is a hot, fist-sized glass bottle that dies like a lightbulb; a transistor is a sliver of silicon with no filament to burn out. Then flip the frame — the transistor's real gift is not speed but SCALE, because you can make billions of them at once by printing. Fun fact for the room: their phone holds more transistors than there are stars in the Milky Way, every one descended from a 1947 lab bench in New Jersey.
S5 · Evolution

The integrated circuit 1958–59

  • Kilby (Texas Instruments) — first working IC, on germanium
  • Noyce (Fairchild) — the practical, plantable silicon version
  • Whole circuits on one chip — wiring becomes printing

Stop assembling computers. Start printing them.

The problem the IC solved was the "tyranny of numbers": a computer needs millions of connections, and humans soldering them by hand was the bottleneck and the reliability killer. Kilby demonstrated the idea (and got the Nobel in 2000); Noyce made it manufacturable and co-founded Intel — a nice case of invention versus engineering. One sentence to plant: once circuits are printed like photographs, making them smaller makes them cheaper, faster AND lower-power simultaneously — which is the engine behind the next slide.
S5 · Evolution

Moore's Law 1965

Transistor count doubles roughly every two years.

Not physics — an observation that became a business plan, and then a self-fulfilling prophecy for 50 years.

🔬 Live demo moore.html — ride the exponential
Open the demo and toggle the log scale — the "straight line" IS the story, because a straight line on a log plot means exponential growth. Stress that Moore's Law is not a law of nature: it was a 1965 extrapolation from four data points that the industry adopted as a shared roadmap, and everyone invested as if it were true, which made it true. Ask the room to estimate the doubling factor over 50 years (2^25 ≈ 33 million times) — nobody's intuition survives contact with an exponential.
S5 · Evolution

The microprocessor & the PC 1971–1980s

  • Intel 4004 1971 — an entire CPU on one chip
  • Altair 8800 1975 — the first personal computer
  • Apple II · PET · TRS-80 — computing lands on desks

The CPU becomes a component — and the computer becomes personal.

The 4004 was designed for a Japanese desk calculator — Intel talked the client into a general-purpose chip instead of custom logic, accidentally creating the microprocessor market. The Altair shipped as a kit with switches and lights, no keyboard, no screen — and sold thousands in weeks, which told everyone the demand was real; two readers named Gates and Allen wrote its BASIC. Note the architectural inversion for the room: for the first time the CPU is a cheap standard part and the VALUE moves to software.
S5 · Evolution

The clock plateau early 2000s

  • Heat and power end the GHz race — ~4 GHz, then a wall
  • The answer is parallel: multicore, GPUs, clusters, accelerators
  • Free speed is over — software must now find the parallelism

"The free lunch is over." — the day architecture became everyone's problem.

Explain the wall in one line: power grows roughly with the cube of clock frequency, so past ~4 GHz a CPU approaches the power density of a nuclear reactor's core — physics said no. The industry's pivot: instead of one faster core, ship two, then four, then sixty-four slower ones. The consequence students must feel: before 2004 old programs got faster with each new chip automatically; after it, only parallel programs improve — which is why this course exists and why their code cares about threads.
S6 · Modern era

CISC vs RISC

CISC — x86

  • Many complex instructions
  • Multi-cycle; hardware does the work

RISC — ARM, SPARC, PowerPC

  • Few simple single-cycle instructions
  • Pipelines + the compiler does the work

Patterson & Ditzel, 1980. Today: RISC cores behind CISC front-ends.

🔬 Live demo cisc-risc.html
Use the kitchen analogy: CISC is a food processor with a button per recipe; RISC is a sharp knife and technique — fewer tools, used faster. The 1980 Patterson & Ditzel insight was empirical: compilers mostly used the simple instructions anyway, so silicon spent on complex ones was wasted. Resolve the war honestly with the demo: modern x86 chips translate CISC instructions into internal RISC-like micro-ops, so the answer to "who won?" is "both — RISC inside, CISC outside," and their phone (ARM) versus laptop (x86) question from slide 3 is now answered.
S6 · Modern era

The networked computer

  • Internet + TCP/IP + the Web — machines stop being islands
  • Computation becomes distributed across many systems
  • The architecture question grows: not one CPU, but thousands cooperating

"The network is the computer."

The shift to voice: for forty years "computer architecture" meant one box, and networking quietly dissolved the box — a Google search runs on more machines in 0.2 seconds than existed on Earth in 1960. TCP/IP is an architecture lesson in itself: a fixed, minimal interface (like an ISA) with endless freedom of implementation beneath it, which is why 1970s protocols still run today's Web. Ask where a Google search "happens" — the room will realize there is no single place, and that is the point.
S6 · Modern era

Mobile & embedded

  • Constraint flips: not speed, but power, heat, battery
  • Specialized silicon: signal processors, camera ISPs, NPUs
  • Real-time guarantees — the airbag cannot buffer

The architecture in your pocket outruns ENIAC a billion times over — on a battery.

Get the census done live: ask everyone to count the computers they carried into the room — phone, watch, earbuds, laptop — then add the invisible ones (a modern car ships with 50–100 processors; even a phone charger contains one). Embedded machines outnumber PCs enormously; the "typical computer" has no screen and no keyboard. Land the constraint flip: on a battery, performance-per-watt replaces raw performance as the design goal — a different optimization target produces a different architecture, which is exactly the organization lesson from slide 4.
S6 · Modern era

Cloud & virtualization

  • Virtualization: one physical machine hosts many simulated ones
  • Cloud: on-demand, elastic resources — computing as a utility (IaaS)
  • Rent a supercomputer by the minute

The stored-program idea, one level up: even the machine is now software.

Connect it back deliberately: von Neumann made programs into data; virtualization makes entire MACHINES into data — a VM is a file you can copy, pause and email. The economics is the second punch: before the cloud a startup bought servers up front, now it rents a thousand cores for an hour for the price of a pizza, and elasticity changed who gets to build big systems. Preempt the misconception that "the cloud" is fluffy — it is warehouses of very physical machines, and someone architects every rack.
S6 · Modern era

Edge computing

  • Process near the data source, not in a distant datacenter
  • Why: latency — light is fast, but not fast enough
  • Enablers: 5G, IoT, capable tiny devices

The pendulum swings back: from the mainframe to the cloud… and out to the edge.

Make latency physical: a round trip to a datacenter 1,500 km away costs ~20–30 ms even at light speed with perfect networks — fine for email, fatal for a self-driving car deciding whether to brake. Show them the pendulum: centralized mainframes → personal PCs → centralized cloud → distributed edge; computing keeps oscillating between "put it in one big place" and "put it where the action is." Ask for examples they already use — voice assistants doing wake-word detection on-device is edge computing in their pocket right now.
S6 · Modern era

Quantum computing

  • Qubits: 0, 1 — or a superposition of both; plus entanglement
  • Known wins: Shor (factoring), Grover (search)
  • NISQ era: fragile qubits, error correction still open

Not a faster computer — a different kind of computation.

Kill the biggest misconception first: a quantum computer is NOT "a computer that tries all answers at once" — it choreographs interference so wrong answers cancel and right ones amplify, which only works for special problem structures (hence Shor and Grover, not "everything"). Be honest about NISQ: today's qubits decohere in microseconds and error correction may need a thousand physical qubits per logical one — we are in the vacuum-tube era of quantum. Close the loop: it took 70 years from ENIAC to your phone; ask the room where quantum's ENIAC moment is on that clock.
S7 · Conclusion

Recap

  • Architecture = what the programmer sees; organization = how it's built
  • The arc: automation → electronics → programmability → stored program
  • Moore's Law drove 50 years — then the plateau made parallelism mandatory
  • Now: RISC-hearted CPUs, networks, cloud, edge — and quantum on the horizon
Run this as retrieval practice, not a lecture: ask the room to reconstruct each bullet before you reveal your phrasing — "what was the exam distinction?", "what were the four steps of the arc?". If time is short, sacrifice anything on this slide except the architecture-versus-organization line; it anchors the quiz. Remind them the interactive lecture page covers everything here at their own pace.
S7 · Conclusion

Why history matters

Every "new" idea in architecture is an old idea whose technology finally arrived.

  • Paradigm shifts to watch for: stored program · ICs · RISC · cloud
  • The next shift will look obvious — afterwards
Prove the claim with cases they now know: Babbage designed a general-purpose computer 110 years before the hardware could exist; Lovelace described software in 1843; neural networks waited from the 1950s until GPUs arrived. The skill this course builds is recognizing which "impossible" ideas are merely early — that is what made von Neumann, Patterson and the cloud pioneers right. Send them off with the thought that someone in this room will be deciding what the post-Moore machine looks like.
