ENIAC — programming with a room full of cables

The first general-purpose all-electronic computer: 17,468 vacuum tubes doing 5,000 additions a second — but its “program” was the wiring itself. Below is a working plugboard machine. Drag cables between jacks (thin red = program pulses, thick blue = data trunks), press GO, and watch a pulse fly from unit to unit while ring counters count at (slowed-down) electronic speed.

The machine

1945 · 17,468 tubes · 30 tons

The Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer, built at the University of Pennsylvania — completed 1945, unveiled February 1946. The first general-purpose, all-electronic digital computer: 17,468 vacuum tubes, ~30 tons, 150 kW, ~5,000 additions/second — about 1,000× faster than the electromechanical machines before it. It was decimal: each digit lived in a ring counter, a circle of ten tube stages with exactly one “on”.

Programmed by plugging

No stored instructions. A problem was “set up” by plugging cables between units and setting switches — days to weeks per problem, planned first on paper setup diagrams. The first programmers were six women: Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas and Ruth Lichterman — they worked out the setups and debugged the machine down to individual tubes.

Why it was built

Artillery firing tables for the Army’s Ballistic Research Lab. One 60-second trajectory took a human “computer” ~20 hours; ENIAC did it in ~30 seconds — and a table needed ~3,000 trajectories. Its success led straight to the stored-program idea: von Neumann’s 1945 EDVAC report was written from inside this project.

    Example setups — one click plugs the board

    ① Running total: 1+2+…+10 = 55

    Each loop the Constant Transmitter pushes +1 into ACC 1 (the counter i), then ACC 2 receives ACC 1’s value. Accumulators accumulate — addition is free-running electronics, and the whole 10-loop run is 20 add-times ≈ 4 ms of real ENIAC time: this loop finishes faster than you can blink.

    ② THE job: artillery trajectory (Euler steps)

    A simplified vertical launch: each step, altitude y grows by v (ACC 1→ACC 2), then v loses g·Δt = 10 via ACC 3’s subtractive port. Every ACC 2 value is one row of a firing table. This integration — with drag and elevation angles — is why ENIAC exists: one 60-second trajectory took a human ~20 hours, ENIAC ~30 seconds; a full table had ~3,000 trajectories.

    ③ Squares by odd numbers: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25

    Squares differ by successive odd numbers, so adding the odd-number generator (ACC 1, starting at 1, fed +2 each loop) into ACC 2 logs the squares — pure addition, the same finite-difference trick Babbage’s Difference Engine used a century earlier.

    Simplifications — where this model cheats

    • 4 accumulators of 6 digits here vs the real 20 accumulators of 10 digits (plus sign).
    • No multiplier or divider/square-rooter units — the real ENIAC had dedicated ones.
    • The Master Programmer is reduced to one loop counter; the real unit had 10 decade counters driving six-stage steppers and could nest and sequence whole program branches.
    • Our blue trunks carry “a number”; real digit trunks carried trains of 100 kHz pulses (a 7 was seven pulses on that digit line), with separate carry-over circuitry.
    • Subtraction here steps rings backward for clarity; the real machine only ever advanced rings and subtracted by adding ten’s-complements (you can still see complements when a value goes negative).
    • Presets preload accumulator values directly; real operators cleared and loaded them through the constant transmitter and IBM card reader.
    • Our jacks politely refuse bad plugs; real setups were checked by hand — and still failed on dud tubes and bad joints.

    Electronic speed, human-speed programming

    ENIAC ran at electronic speed but was programmed at human speed — days of plugging for seconds of computing. Notice how that asymmetry is exactly what the stored-program concept fixed: make the program data in memory, and “replugging” becomes reloading in milliseconds. The EDVAC memo proposing it was written from inside this very room.

    Plugboards reborn

    Wiring up dataflow is not dead. FPGAs, systolic arrays and TPU pipelines are plugboards reborn: when the problem is fixed, wiring beats fetching — no instruction fetch, no decode, just data streaming through configured hardware. ENIAC’s “setup” is a modern accelerator’s bitstream.

    Programming was born hands-on — and female

    McNulty, Jennings, Snyder, Wescoff, Bilas and Lichterman invented debugging at the panel — tracing a wrong digit through cables and steppers down to a single dead vacuum tube among 17,468. Programming was born as a hands-on engineering discipline, and it was born female.
