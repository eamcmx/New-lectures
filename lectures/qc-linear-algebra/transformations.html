Linear Operators — matrices move space

Type a 2×2 matrix (or pick a preset chip), then press Apply and watch the whole grid morph. The blue and yellow arrows are the basis vectors î and ĵ — the matrix's columns tell you exactly where they land. Turn on compose to stack transformations one on top of another.

With compose on, pressing Apply multiplies the new matrix onto whatever is already on screen: the readout accumulates B·A — first A, then B. Order matters!

Aside: Pauli-Y lives in the complex part

0−i i0

The entries of Y are imaginary, so it rotates through the complex parts of the amplitudes — invisible in this real-plane picture, but fully visible as a rotation about the y-axis on the Bloch sphere.

Columns = landing spots

Notice how the blue column of the matrix is exactly where î lands, and the yellow column is where ĵ lands. A linear operator is fully determined by what it does to a basis — so you can read any matrix by asking "where do î and ĵ go?"

Order matters: BA ≠ AB

Turn compose on and try Rotation 90° then Shear, then Reset and do Shear then Rotation 90°. The grids end up in different places! BA means "apply A first, then B" — matrix products read right-to-left, and swapping them usually changes the answer.

Quantum gates are unitary

Apply Pauli X, Z, or Hadamard and watch: the grid turns or flips but never stretches — every vector keeps its length. This is why quantum gates must be unitary operators: they rotate state space without stretching it, so the probabilities |α|² + |β|² keep summing to 1.
