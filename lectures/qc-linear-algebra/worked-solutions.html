E1 Complex arithmetic

For z = 1 + 2i and w = 3 − i, compute z + w,  zw,  z / w and |z|.

Try first: all four are one-line moves from the definitions — do them on paper, then check yourself step by step.

Step 1
z + w = (1 + 3) + (2 − 1)i = 4 + i
Addition is componentwise: real parts add, imaginary parts add — exactly like a 2-D vector sum.
Step 2
zw = (1 + 2i)(3 − i) = 3 − i + 6i − 2i² = 3 + 5i + 2 = 5 + 5i
Expand like any product of binomials, then use the one new rule: i² = −1, so −2i² = +2.
Step 3
z / w = (1 + 2i)/(3 − i) · (3 + i)/(3 + i) — multiply top and bottom by the conjugate w̄ = 3 + i.
Multiplying by w̄/w̄ changes nothing (it’s 1) but makes the denominator real: ww̄ = |w|².
Step 4
Numerator: (1 + 2i)(3 + i) = 3 + i + 6i + 2i² = 1 + 7i.  Denominator: (3 − i)(3 + i) = 9 + 1 = 10. So z / w = (1 + 7i)/10 = 0.1 + 0.7i.
The denominator is 3² + 1² — a difference of squares with i² = −1 always yields a² + b², a real number.
Step 5
|z| = √(1² + 2²) = √5
The modulus is the distance from the origin in the complex plane — Pythagoras on the real and imaginary parts.
Final answer z + w = 4 + i,    zw = 5 + 5i,    z / w = 0.1 + 0.7i,    |z| = √5

E2 Polar form & De Moivre

Write z = 1 + i√3 in polar and exponential form, then compute z⁶ via De Moivre.

Try first: sketch the point (1, √3) in the plane — the angle should be a famous one.

Step 1
r = |z| = √(1² + (√3)²) = √(1 + 3) = √4 = 2
The modulus is always the first ingredient of polar form — the length of the arrow.
Step 2
θ = arctan(√3 / 1) = π/3  (the point sits in the first quadrant, so no correction needed).
tan θ = (imaginary part)/(real part); √3 is the tangent of 60° = π/3. Always check the quadrant before trusting arctan.
Step 3
z = 2(cos π/3 + i sin π/3) = 2eiπ/3
Euler’s formula eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ compresses polar form into a single exponential.
Step 4
z⁶ = (2eiπ/3)⁶ = 2⁶ ei·6·π/3 = 64 ei2π
De Moivre: raising to a power raises the modulus to that power and multiplies the angle. Six sixty-degree turns.
Step 5
ei2π = 1, so z⁶ = 64 — a real, positive number.
A full turn of 2π lands back on the positive real axis. Six copies of π/3 make exactly one revolution.
Final answer z = 2(cos π/3 + i sin π/3) = 2eiπ/3,    z⁶ = 64

E3 Normalize & check orthogonality

For |u⟩ = (1, 1)ᵀ and |v⟩ = (1, −1)ᵀ: normalize both, compute ⟨u|v⟩, and conclude they form an orthonormal basis.

Try first: compute the two norms and the inner product on paper — three tiny calculations.

Step 1
‖u‖ = √⟨u|u⟩ = √(1² + 1²) = √2,  and likewise ‖v‖ = √(1² + (−1)²) = √2.
The norm comes from the inner product of a vector with itself — the same √(sum of squares) as ordinary length.
Step 2
û = (1/√2)(1, 1)ᵀ,  v̂ = (1/√2)(1, −1)ᵀ
Dividing a vector by its own norm always produces a unit vector — direction kept, length rescaled to 1.
Step 3
⟨u|v⟩ = 1·1 + 1·(−1) = 0  → orthogonal. (Entries are real, so conjugating the bra changes nothing.)
Inner product zero is the definition of orthogonality — the two directions carry no shadow of each other.
Step 4
Two orthonormal vectors in a 2-dimensional space are automatically linearly independent, so they form an orthonormal basis of ℝ². In quantum notation these are exactly |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2.
Orthogonality forces independence, and n independent vectors in an n-dimensional space always span it. The |±⟩ pair is the measurement basis of the Pauli X.
Final answer û = (1/√2)(1, 1)ᵀ,   v̂ = (1/√2)(1, −1)ᵀ,   ⟨u|v⟩ = 0  — an orthonormal basis: the states |+⟩ and |−⟩.

E4 Eigen-everything

For A = 3113: find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, then write the spectral decomposition.

Try first: set up det(A − λI) = 0 yourself — the quadratic factors nicely.

Step 1
det(A − λI) = (3 − λ)(3 − λ) − 1·1 = (3 − λ)² − 1
Eigenvalues are exactly the λ that make A − λI singular — determinant zero.
Step 2
λ² − 6λ + 8 = 0  ⇒  (λ − 4)(λ − 2) = 0  ⇒  λ₁ = 4,  λ₂ = 2
Expand and factor the characteristic polynomial. Both roots are real — no surprise, since A is Hermitian (real symmetric).
Step 3
For λ₁ = 4: (A − 4I)v = 0 with A − 4I = −111−1, i.e. −v₁ + v₂ = 0 ⇒ v₂ = v₁. Normalized: |v₁⟩ = (1/√2)(1, 1)ᵀ.
An eigenvector is any nonzero solution of (A − λI)v = 0; we normalize because the spectral decomposition wants unit vectors.
Step 4
For λ₂ = 2: A − 2I = 1111, i.e. v₁ + v₂ = 0 ⇒ v₂ = −v₁. Normalized: |v₂⟩ = (1/√2)(1, −1)ᵀ.
Notice ⟨v₁|v₂⟩ = 0 — eigenvectors of a Hermitian matrix for distinct eigenvalues are always orthogonal. (They’re E3’s |+⟩, |−⟩!)
Step 5
Outer products: 4|v₁⟩⟨v₁| = 4 · ½ 1111 = 2222,   2|v₂⟩⟨v₂| = 2 · ½ 1−1−11 = 1−1−11
The outer product |v⟩⟨v| of a unit vector is the projector onto its line; each eigenvalue weights its own projector.
Step 6
Sum: 2222 + 1−1−11 = 3113 = A  ✓
The check is the theorem: every Hermitian operator equals the eigenvalue-weighted sum of its eigen-projectors, A = Σ λⱼ |j⟩⟨j|.
Final answer λ₁ = 4, |v₁⟩ = (1/√2)(1, 1)ᵀ;   λ₂ = 2, |v₂⟩ = (1/√2)(1, −1)ᵀ;    A = 4|v₁⟩⟨v₁| + 2|v₂⟩⟨v₂|

E5 Kronecker product & a 2-qubit state

Compute X ⊗ Z for the Paulis X = 0110, Z = 100−1, then apply it to |01⟩ = (0, 1, 0, 0)ᵀ.

Try first: before any 4×4 arithmetic, predict the outcome qubit-by-qubit: what do X and Z each do to their own qubit?

Step 1
Kronecker rule: each entry of X multiplies a full copy of Z: X ⊗ Z = 0·Z1·Z1·Z0·Z  (a 2×2 grid of 2×2 blocks).
That is the definition of the Kronecker product: the left matrix sets the block layout, the right matrix fills each block.
Step 2
X ⊗ Z = 0010000−110000−100
Top-left and bottom-right blocks are 0·Z = zeros; top-right and bottom-left are copies of Z.
Step 3
|01⟩ = |0⟩ ⊗ |1⟩ = (1, 0)ᵀ ⊗ (0, 1)ᵀ = (0, 1, 0, 0)ᵀ — the second basis vector of ℂ⁴.
Two-qubit basis states are Kronecker products of single-qubit ones; |01⟩ lives in slot 2 (binary 01 = index 1, counting from 0).
Step 4
Multiplying a matrix by a basis vector just selects a column — here column 2: (X ⊗ Z)|01⟩ = (0, 0, 0, −1)ᵀ = −|11⟩.
A standard basis vector with a 1 in slot k picks out column k of any matrix — no full multiplication needed.
Step 5
Sanity check the sign, qubit by qubit: (X ⊗ Z)(|0⟩ ⊗ |1⟩) = X|0⟩ ⊗ Z|1⟩ = |1⟩ ⊗ (−|1⟩) = −|11⟩  ✓
(A ⊗ B)(|u⟩ ⊗ |v⟩) = A|u⟩ ⊗ B|v⟩ — tensored operators act on their own factors. X flips |0⟩ → |1⟩; Z tags |1⟩ with −1.
Final answer X ⊗ Z = 0010000−110000−100,    (X ⊗ Z)|01⟩ = (0, 0, 0, −1)ᵀ = −|11⟩
