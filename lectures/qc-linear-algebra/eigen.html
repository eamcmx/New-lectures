Eigenvectors & Eigenvalues — the directions that survive

Drag the yellow dot around the unit circle and watch the blue vector Av swing around. Most directions get knocked off their own line — but a few special ones don't. When v lands on such a direction, Av lines up exactly: Av = λv. Those are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factor λ is the eigenvalue. (The zero vector is excluded — it trivially satisfies the equation — but λ = 0 itself is perfectly legal.)

A = λ₁|e₁⟩⟨e₁| + λ₂|e₂⟩⟨e₂| — this matrix is symmetric (Hermitian on ℝ²), so it has a spectral decomposition: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors. For distinct eigenvalues of a Hermitian or unitary operator, the eigen-directions are always perpendicular — check the dashed lines.

Characteristic polynomial — where det(A − λI) = 0

A direction that survives

Notice how an eigenvector doesn't get knocked off its own line — the transformation only stretches (or flips, or squishes) it. Av = λv says the output stays on the input's span; all the matrix can do there is multiply by a number. That number is the eigenvalue λ.

Why det(A − λI) = 0?

Av = λv rearranges to (A − λI)v = 0 with v ≠ 0. That asks: for which λ does A − λI squish some direction all the way to zero? Exactly when its determinant vanishes. For an n×n matrix this is a degree-n polynomial — so at most n eigenvalues.

Measurement = eigenvalues

In quantum mechanics you never "see" the state — you see eigenvalues. Measuring Pauli Z can only return its eigenvalues +1 or −1, and the state collapses onto the matching eigenvector |0⟩ or |1⟩. This is why Hermitian operators (real eigenvalues!) represent observables.
