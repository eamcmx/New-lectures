Quantum computing lives in ℂⁿ — amplitudes are complex numbers — but the geometry of span is the same story you see here in ℝ²: a set of vectors either reaches everything, or it collapses onto something thinner.

Span = every place you can reach

The span of |v₁⟩, |v₂⟩ is the set of all combinations a₁|v₁⟩ + a₂|v₂⟩. Notice how, with two independent directions, sliding a₁ and a₂ lets the green arrow reach anywhere in the plane. A set that reaches all of V is called a spanning set: every v ∈ V is writable as Σ aₖ|vₖ⟩.

Independence = no wasted vector

The moment |v₂⟩ falls onto |v₁⟩'s line, it stops contributing a new direction — it's just a multiple of the other, and the span snaps down to a line. Linearly independent means no vector is a combination of the others. A basis is exactly a linearly independent spanning set: a minimal, complete coordinate system. The number of basis vectors is the dimension — and many different bases describe the same space.

The computational basis of ℂ²

In quantum computing the standard basis of ℂ² is |0⟩ = (1, 0) and |1⟩ = (0, 1) — the "computational basis". Every qubit state is one recipe in this basis: |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. This is why a basis matters: because it's a basis, that recipe (α, β) exists and is unique for every state.
