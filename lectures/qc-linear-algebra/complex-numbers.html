The Complex Plane — one number, four costumes

Drag the blue dot to move z around the plane, and watch the very same number dress up in four equivalent notations below. Then scroll down to see why multiplying complex numbers is really a rotate-and-scale.

Drag the blue point. a = real part, b = imaginary part, r = |z|, θ = angle from the real axis.
Algebraic
z = 0 + 0i
Polar
z = 0(cos 0 + i·sin 0)
Exponential
z = 0·ei·0  (0°)
Matrix
z ↔ 00 00
z* = 0 − 0i   and   z·z* = a² + b² = 0 = |z|²  — purely real, the imaginary parts cancel.

Multiplication = rotate & scale

Moduli multiply, angles add.
zw = (ax − by) + (ay + bx)i = …
|zw| = |z|·|w| = 0 × 0 = 0
arg(zw) = θz + θw = 0 + 0 = 0 rad

Why four costumes?

Each notation makes a different job easy. Algebraic a + bi shines for adding; polar and exponential r·eiθ shine for multiplying (multiply the r's, add the θ's); and the 2×2 matrix form reveals the secret: a complex number literally is a rotation-plus-scaling of the plane.

Conjugation flips the phase

Notice how z* is just z mirrored across the real axis — same modulus, opposite angle: r·e−iθ. So in the product z·z* the phases cancel (θ − θ = 0) and you're left with the purely real number r² = |z|². This is why measurement probabilities |α|² in quantum mechanics are always real and non-negative.

Rotation is the heartbeat of quantum gates

Multiplying by a unit complex number eiθ rotates without stretching — and that is exactly what quantum gates do to amplitudes. Phase gates like S and T multiply the amplitude of |1⟩ by eiθ, quietly rotating it in this very plane. Interference — the whole magic of quantum algorithms — is these rotated arrows adding up or cancelling.
