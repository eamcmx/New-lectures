The Qubit — a unit vector in ℂ²

A qubit is nothing more exotic than a unit vector in ℂ²: |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ with |α|² + |β|² = 1. Drag the green arrow around the circle, slide the phase, then hit Measure! and watch the state collapse onto a basis vector.

The real slice of state space: horizontal axis is |0⟩, vertical is |1⟩. You can only drag along the circle — that constraint is the normalization |α|²+|β|²=1. The dashed projections are the amplitudes α and β, and the translucent squares drawn on them have areas |α|² and |β|² — by Pythagoras the two areas always sum to exactly 1.

Green ticks mark the theoretical probabilities |α|², |β|². Run enough measurements and the observed frequencies crowd toward them — that's the Born rule showing up as statistics. (“Measure ×100” samples 100 fresh copies of the state; a single “Measure!” collapses this one.)

Normalization = living on the circle

Notice how you never chose to satisfy |α|²+|β|²=1 — the drag simply wouldn't let you leave the unit circle (the unit sphere, once amplitudes go complex). That's all normalization is: the state has length 1 in the inner-product norm from the previous page, so its squared components are probabilities waiting to happen.

Measurement is projection

When you hit Measure!, the arrow snaps onto |0⟩ or |1⟩ — it gets projected onto a basis vector of the measurement operator. The eigenvalue is the number you read out; the collapse is the projection. The dashed lines you've been watching were the projections all along.

A bit picks; a qubit points

A classical bit chooses 0 or 1. A qubit holds a whole direction in ℂ² — including an invisible relative phase. But measurement only ever hands you one basis outcome, with the amplitudes α, β deciding the odds. This is why quantum algorithms choreograph phases before daring to measure.
