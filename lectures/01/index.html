Lecture 01 Module 1 Robot Dynamics

From Kinematics to Dynamics

Why forces and torques matter, and how the equation $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ ties it all together.

You already know how to describe where a robot is and how it moves — that's kinematics. But the moment you ask what does it cost to make that motion happen, you've stepped into dynamics. In this lecture we'll make that transition concrete: why forces and torques matter, where coupling and gravity sneak in, and the one equation that quietly runs every serious robot controller. By the end you should see why nobody builds a real robot on kinematics alone.

What this lecture covers

This module bridges the gap between kinematics (describing motion) and dynamics (explaining what causes it). We'll work through five connected ideas: (1) why kinematics alone isn't enough for real robots, (2) the practical stakes of dynamic modeling in manufacturing and medical robotics, (3) the split between forward and inverse dynamics, (4) the mathematical framework built around the equation $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$, and (5) how all of this feeds directly into modern robot control architectures.

Think of it as the why lecture: the rest of the course will derive and apply this equation in detail, but first we need to agree on why it's worth the effort.

Kinematics tells you the route. Dynamics tells you what the journey costs.

§ 1

From kinematics to dynamics: the essential transition

Kinematics gives you the geometric backbone — forward/inverse position, Jacobians, velocity and acceleration relationships. It answers what is moving and how, but it cannot tell you why.

Dynamics adds the missing layer: what forces and torques cause this motion? That single shift in question changes everything. Suddenly you can answer practical engineering questions — how much torque the motor needs, why the arm overshoots, how a payload changes the required actuator effort.

A useful analogy: kinematics is the map showing the route from A to B. Dynamics tells you how much fuel you'll burn, what engine power you need, and how the terrain pushes back. Both are necessary, but only dynamics makes the journey physically real.

Every dynamic analysis starts with kinematic quantities — positions, velocities, accelerations. Dynamics doesn't replace kinematics; it contains it.

Key contrasts

Kinematics uses linear algebra and transformations on $(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}, \ddot{\mathbf{q}})$. Dynamics adds mass, inertia, friction, forces and torques — and requires Lagrangian mechanics and differential equations to handle the coupling.

Configuration dependency

The effective inertia a motor 'sees' changes dramatically with posture. A motor that easily moves an extended arm may stall when the arm folds — same hardware, different physics.

§ 2

Why dynamic modeling is critical, not optional

The gap between a robot that works in the lab and one that performs reliably in industry usually comes down to the quality of the dynamic model. Without it you can't size actuators, design controllers, or optimize energy use — you're guessing.

Four physical effects make this non-trivial:

  • Inertial coupling. Accelerating joint 1 also accelerates every link downstream of it. The effective load changes with posture.
  • Gravitational loading. A horizontal link sees maximum gravity torque; a vertical one sees almost none. This shifts continuously as the robot moves.
  • Velocity-dependent forces. Coriolis and centrifugal terms appear whenever joints move simultaneously — invisible in statics, dominant at speed.
  • Configuration dependency. The effective inertia at each actuator is a function of $\mathbf{q}$.

Newton's $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ still rules, but in a multi-link manipulator it becomes a system of coupled nonlinear differential equations — which is exactly why intuitive control approaches (just crank up the torque, slap on a PID) fail as soon as speed or precision climb.

The physics behind the necessity

Inertial coupling, gravity, Coriolis/centrifugal, and configuration dependency together make a robot a system of coupled nonlinear ODEs — not a collection of independent joints.

Two flagship application domains

Precision manufacturing needs dynamics to compensate inertial forces, minimize vibration, and keep performance consistent across payloads. Medical & surgical robotics needs it for force feedback, tremor-free motion, tissue adaptation, and safety-critical force limiting.

WatchDrive a 2-link planar arm through a sweep and watch the four dynamic effects light up in turn — inertial coupling, gravity torque, Coriolis only-when-both-move, and an order-of-magnitude swing in effective inertia between folded and extended.

§ 3

Case study: automotive welding robot

A 6-DOF arm welds car body panels at up to 1.5 m/s with ±0.1 mm accuracy. Three dynamic effects must be tamed:

1. Inertial effects during high-speed motion. Rapid acceleration produces large inertial forces; pure feedback can't react fast enough. Solution: feedforward control via inverse dynamics computes the torque required for the desired acceleration before tracking error appears.

2. Gravitational compensation. An extended horizontal arm sags under its own weight — easily several millimetres, well outside tolerance. Solution: compute $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ in real time and apply it continuously so the robot 'feels weightless' to the position loop.

3. Velocity-dependent effects. Coriolis and centrifugal forces deflect the tool on curved paths and can consume 20–30 % of actuator capacity at speed. Solution: velocity feedforward using $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$.

Quantitative impact: accuracy improved from ±0.5 mm to ±0.05 mm (10×), production speed up 40 %, energy down 25 %, scrap rates down 85 %. The dynamic modeling investment paid back in six months.

This is the lecture's punchline: a feedback-only controller would have lost this race. Feedforward from a good dynamic model is what hits ±0.05 mm at 1.5 m/s.

Feedforward torque

$\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ff}} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — predict what the physics needs, then let feedback handle only the unmodeled bits.

§ 4

Forward vs. inverse dynamics

Robot dynamics problems split cleanly into two complementary classes — same physics, opposite directions.

Forward dynamics is prediction: given torques $\boldsymbol{\tau}(t)$, find the resulting accelerations $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}(t)$. Used for simulation, what-if analysis, safety monitoring.

Inverse dynamics is planning: given a desired trajectory $\mathbf{q}(t), \dot{\mathbf{q}}(t), \ddot{\mathbf{q}}(t)$, find the torques $\boldsymbol{\tau}(t)$ that will produce it. Used for real-time control, feedforward design, actuator sizing.

Forward dynamics is computationally heavier — it requires inverting $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$. Inverse dynamics is a direct substitution and far cheaper, which is exactly why it's the workhorse inside real-time controllers.

Forward dynamics asks 'if I push, where will it go?' Inverse dynamics asks 'if I want it to go there, how hard do I push?' Real robots use both — inverse for control, forward for verification.

Forward dynamics — given torque, solve for acceleration
q̈ = D⁻¹(q) · [ τ − h(q, q̇) − c(q) ]
Inverse dynamics — given trajectory, solve for torque
τ = D(q) · q̈ + h(q, q̇) + c(q)

Surgical robot example

A 7-DOF cardiac surgery robot uses inverse dynamics pre-op to plan torques, optimize trajectories, and verify safety, then uses forward dynamics intra-op to predict motion, detect unexpected tissue contact, and simulate emergency stops.

§ 5

The fundamental equation and its components

Every robot manipulator's motion is governed by

$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}) \tag{1.1}$$

for an $n$-DOF robot, where $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ is the joint torque vector. Each term has a clear physical identity:

  • $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ — the $n \times n$ inertia matrix. Symmetric and positive definite. Diagonal entries are the effective inertia at each joint; off-diagonals are inertial coupling between joints. It changes with posture.
  • $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ — the $n \times 1$ Coriolis and centrifugal vector. Quadratic in velocity: $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\alpha\dot{\mathbf{q}}) = \alpha^2 \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$. Coriolis terms come from joint-joint interaction; centrifugal terms from self-motion.
  • $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$ — the $n \times 1$ gravitational vector. Position-dependent only, velocity-independent. Conservative, derivable from a potential.

Inertia matrix D(q)

Symmetric, positive definite, posture-dependent. Off-diagonal terms are the reason you cannot tune joints independently.

Coriolis/centrifugal h(q, q̇)

Quadratic in velocity — negligible when slow, dominant when fast. Invisible in static analysis.

Gravity c(q)

Depends only on configuration. Compensating it removes most steady-state error in vertical-plane motion.

§ 6

Mathematical properties that control engineers exploit

Three properties of equation (1.1) underpin most of modern robot control:

1. Symmetry and positive definiteness of $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$.

$$\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{D}^T(\mathbf{q}), \qquad \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q}) \mathbf{x} > 0 \;\; \forall \mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0} \tag{1.2}$$

This guarantees the kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ is always positive — and that $\mathbf{D}^{-1}$ always exists, so forward dynamics is well-posed.

2. Skew-symmetry property. Choosing the Coriolis matrix $\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ appropriately,

$$\dot{\mathbf{D}}(\mathbf{q}) - 2\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) \;\text{is skew-symmetric.} \tag{1.3}$$

This is the passivity property — the basis for almost every stability proof in robot control (computed torque, adaptive control, energy-shaping).

3. Boundedness. $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is bounded above and below for all configurations; $\mathbf{h}$ is bounded by a quadratic in velocity; $\mathbf{c}$ is bounded for all finite configurations. These bounds let you design robust controllers without knowing the model exactly.

These three properties aren't trivia — they're the reason robot control theory has clean stability proofs at all.

Why passivity matters

The skew-symmetry of $\dot{\mathbf{D}} - 2\mathbf{C}$ implies the manipulator behaves as a passive energy system. Pair it with a passive controller and Lyapunov stability falls out almost for free.

§ 7

Where this fits in a real control system

A typical robot control loop puts the dynamic model right at the centre. The architecture has five stages:

  1. Trajectory planning generates the desired motion $\mathbf{q}_d(t), \dot{\mathbf{q}}_d(t), \ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d(t)$.
  2. Inverse dynamics (feedforward) computes $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ff}} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$.
  3. Feedback control computes a correction $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{fb}}$ from tracking error.
  4. Total torque sent to the actuators: $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{total}} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ff}} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{fb}}$.
  5. Forward dynamics (the plant) — the robot responds with actual motion.

Feedforward does the heavy lifting — it cancels predictable nonlinearities (inertia, Coriolis, gravity) so feedback only has to deal with unmodeled disturbances. A weak dynamic model forces feedback to work much harder, and performance suffers accordingly.

Feedforward = physics you know. Feedback = surprises you don't. Good models shrink the feedback's job to nearly nothing.

Computed-torque control law
τ_total = D(q)·q̈_d + h(q, q̇) + c(q)   ← feedforward (inverse dynamics)
        + K_p·(q_d − q) + K_d·(q̇_d − q̇)   ← feedback (PD on tracking error)

Computed torque control

The architecture above is the classical computed torque (or inverse dynamics) controller. Almost every high-performance industrial and research manipulator uses some variant of it.

Step throughWalk one cycle of the computed-torque controller: the planner emits $\mathbf{q}_d, \dot{\mathbf{q}}_d, \ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d$; inverse dynamics turns that into $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ff}}$; PD feedback adds $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{fb}}$; the plant integrates. Toggle feedforward off and watch tracking error explode — that's the whole point of dynamic modelling.

§ 8

Recap and what's next

You now have the conceptual scaffold for everything that follows:

  • Dynamics extends kinematics by adding forces, torques, mass and inertia — and the coupling between them.
  • Forward dynamics simulates; inverse dynamics controls.
  • Every manipulator obeys $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})$, with each term carrying a clean physical meaning.
  • The matrix properties (symmetry, positive definiteness, passivity, boundedness) are what make robust control mathematically tractable.
  • Modern controllers wire inverse dynamics in as feedforward and let feedback mop up the rest.
