Lecture 04Kinematics and Dynamics of Robots

Composite rotations & moving a rigid body

Mixing fixed-frame and mobile-frame motions, then animating a box in MATLAB.

Last time we chained rotations around the fixed axes and watched a single point fly through space. Today we open the door wider: motions can happen with respect to the mobile frame too — the body's own axes. We'll uncover the pre-multiply / post-multiply rule that keeps the bookkeeping honest, code the whole sequence in MATLAB, and finish by drawing a little box (коробка) and spinning it around. By the end, a rigid body is no longer a point — it's eight vertices we can rotate as one.
00
Opening · today's arc

What this lecture covers

We start with a quick recap of basic rotation matrices and how a sequence around the fixed axes composes by pre-multiplication. Then we introduce the twist: motions can also happen with respect to the mobile frame ($u, v, w$). The key new rule — fixed-frame rotations pre-multiply, mobile-frame rotations post-multiply — is what makes the whole machinery work.

With the rule in hand, we walk through a four-motion sequence in MATLAB, animate a fly attached to the moving frame, and then graduate from a single point to a full rigid body. We define a box by its eight vertices, write a karobka function to draw it, and rotate it around the $y$-axis to close the lecture.

If you only remember one thing from today, remember which side of the identity matrix each rotation goes on. Everything else is just multiplication.
why this matters
From single points to joints
Every robotic joint you'll model next semester is a rotation around a mobile axis. This is the lecture that makes that possible.
01
Recap · last week

Basic rotations & the identity

When two coordinate frames coincide — $u$ along $x$, $v$ along $y$, $w$ along $z$ — the rotation matrix between them is the identity:

$$ R \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

If instead the mobile axes point the opposite way ($u$ along $-x$, etc.), every diagonal entry flips sign and we get $-I$. The columns of $R$ are just the projections of $u, v, w$ onto $x, y, z$ — which is why each basic rotation matrix has the form it does.

The three basic rotations around the fixed axes are $R_x(\theta)$, $R_y(\theta)$ and $R_z(\theta)$, and a sequence of motions around the fixed frame composes by pre-multiplying:

$$ R_C \;=\; R_{\text{last}} \,\cdots\, R_2 \cdot R_1 \cdot I $$
geometry
Columns = projections
The first column of $R$ is $u$ written in $xyz$ coordinates. Second column is $v$. Third column is $w$. That's all a rotation matrix really is.
The three basic rotationsmatlab
Rx = [1 0 0; 0 cos(t) -sin(t); 0 sin(t) cos(t)];
Ry = [cos(t) 0 sin(t); 0 1 0; -sin(t) 0 cos(t)];
Rz = [cos(t) -sin(t) 0; sin(t) cos(t) 0; 0 0 1];

Before we chain anything — let's check that the basic matrices feel familiar. Choose an axis below and the matrix snaps into place with the trig terms in the correct cells.

02
Composition · fixed only

A sequence around the fixed frame

Take the worked sequence from class: first rotate around $z$ by $\theta_1$, then around $y$ by $\theta_2$, then around $x$ by $\theta_3$. All three motions are with respect to the fixed frame, so each new rotation slots in on the left of what came before:

$$ R_C \;=\; R_x(\theta_3) \cdot R_y(\theta_2) \cdot R_z(\theta_1) \cdot I $$

The identity on the right is the starting state (frames coincident). Everything that happens with respect to the fixed system pre-multiplies it.

Read the matrix product right-to-left and you're reading the history of the body's motion.
caution
Order matters
Matrix multiplication is not commutative. Swap two rotations and you get a different final orientation — try it in MATLAB if you don't believe it.

Press play and watch the chain assemble itself: at each event, a new fixed-frame matrix snaps onto the left of the running product, and the mobile frame snaps to the new orientation. This is the right-to-left reading of $R_C$ made visible.

03
Transformation · point coords

From UVW back to XYZ

A point $A$ attached to the mobile frame has constant coordinates $A_{uvw}$. To know where that point actually is in the fixed frame, we apply the composite rotation matrix:

$$ A_{xyz} \;=\; R_C \cdot A_{uvw} $$

In the demo, $A_{uvw} = (0.3,\ 0.4,\ 0.5)^\top$ and we watched it traced out in the fixed frame as the mobile frame rotated. The on-screen readout simply printed the current $A_{xyz}$ — the same point, expressed in the language of the lab.

metaphor
The fly
Imagine a fly that's glued to the mobile frame. The fly never moves relative to $u, v, w$. But to someone standing in the fixed frame, it traces a curve through space.
Transforming the point each framematlab
A_uvw = [0.3; 0.4; 0.5];
A_xyz = R_C * A_uvw;
04
The twist

Two kinds of motion — fixed vs mobile

Up to now we've only rotated around the fixed axes $x, y, z$. But a body can also rotate around its own axes $u, v, w$ — the mobile frame. The MATLAB demo lets you toggle between them, and the on-screen behaviour is visibly different even for the same numerical angle.

A crucial observation: the matrix that represents a rotation around $u$ has exactly the same form as the one around $x$ — and the same for $v \leftrightarrow y$ and $w \leftrightarrow z$. So we don't need new matrices. What changes is where the matrix goes in the product.

The matrix doesn't care which frame it lives in — but the product does.
equivalence
Same numbers, different placement
$R_u(\theta) \equiv R_x(\theta), \quad R_v(\theta) \equiv R_y(\theta), \quad R_w(\theta) \equiv R_z(\theta)$. Same numbers — different meaning depending on which side of the product you place them.
05
The rule · keystone of the lecture

Pre-multiply or post-multiply

Start from the identity matrix $I$. Now apply two rules consistently:

L

Every motion around the fixed frame ($x$, $y$, or $z$) goes on the left of $I$ — pre-multiplies the current state.

R

Every motion around the mobile frame ($u$, $v$, or $w$) goes on the right of $I$ — post-multiplies the current state.

For the in-class sequence — $R_y(\theta_1)$ fixed, then $R_u(\theta_2)$ mobile, then $R_w(\theta_3)$ mobile, then $R_z(\theta_4)$ fixed — the composite is:

$$ R_C \;=\; R_z(\theta_4) \cdot R_y(\theta_1) \cdot I \cdot R_u(\theta_2) \cdot R_w(\theta_3) $$

Notice how the fixed-frame rotations migrated to the left and the mobile-frame rotations migrated to the right, in the order they happened.

Identity in the middle is your anchor. Fixed left, mobile right. That's the whole game.
common mistake
Mobile on the wrong side
Putting a mobile-frame rotation on the left will give you a syntactically valid matrix that animates wrongly. The numbers won't catch the error — only the animation will.

Time to internalise the rule. Drag each motion onto the workbench in the order it happens; fixed rotations slot to the left of $I$, mobile rotations slot to the right. Drop one on the wrong side and the widget pushes back. End goal: reconstruct $R_C = R_z(\theta_4) \cdot R_y(\theta_1) \cdot I \cdot R_u(\theta_2) \cdot R_w(\theta_3)$.

06
In the IDE

Coding the mixed sequence in MATLAB

We build up the composite matrix one motion at a time, tagging each intermediate state $R_{C1}, R_{C2}, R_{C3}, R_{C4}$. The first motion (fixed $y$) pre-multiplies the identity. The second (mobile $u$) post-multiplies the result. The third (mobile $w$) post-multiplies again. The fourth (fixed $z$) pre-multiplies the lot.

At every step we recompute $A_{xyz} = R_{Ci} \cdot A_{uvw}$ and redraw the mobile frame so the fly's path stays in sync.

One typo I made in class: I wrote RC2 = ... * RC2 instead of RC1. MATLAB rightly complained. Watch your indices.
debugging
Unrecognized variable
If MATLAB says "unrecognized variable RC2", it's because you referenced a state you haven't built yet. Name your intermediates clearly and the bug becomes obvious.
Building R_C step by stepmatlab
% Event 1 — fixed Y
Ry   = [cos(t1) 0 sin(t1); 0 1 0; -sin(t1) 0 cos(t1)];
RC1  = Ry * eye(3);

% Event 2 — mobile U (post-multiply)
Ru   = [1 0 0; 0 cos(t2) -sin(t2); 0 sin(t2) cos(t2)];
RC2  = RC1 * Ru;

% Event 3 — mobile W (post-multiply)
Rw   = [cos(t3) -sin(t3) 0; sin(t3) cos(t3) 0; 0 0 1];
RC3  = RC2 * Rw;

% Event 4 — fixed Z (pre-multiply)
Rz   = [cos(t4) -sin(t4) 0; sin(t4) cos(t4) 0; 0 0 1];
RC4  = Rz * RC3;

A_xyz = RC4 * A_uvw;

Now step the four events live. Press next at each beat: the matching code line lights up, the mobile frame rotates, and the fly at $(0.3,\,0.4,\,0.5)_{uvw}$ re-projects into XYZ. This is the script we wrote in class, line for line.

07
Rigid bodies · 8 vertices

From point to rigid body — the коробка

A rigid body is a set of points whose mutual distances never change. The simplest non-trivial one is a box defined by eight vertices and twelve edges. Pick a box of size $6 \times 4 \times 3$ along $u, v, w$ and the vertex coordinates fall out:

A(0, 0, 0)
B(0, 0, 3)
C(0, 4, 3)
D(0, 4, 0)
E(6, 0, 3)
F(6, 0, 0)
G(6, 4, 0)
H(6, 4, 3)

The twelve edges connect them: four around the back face ($AB, BC, CD, DA$), four around the front face ($EF, FG, GH, HE$), and four spanning struts ($AF, BE, CH, DG$).

The thing about a rigid body is that one rotation matrix rotates every vertex. You don't transform a body — you transform its points, all at once.
mental model
Welded skeleton
Picture the 8 vertices as labeled nodes and each of the 12 edges as a welded strut. Rotating the parent frame should make all 8 nodes and 12 edges move together as one rigid lattice — never bending, never stretching.
karobka.m — draws all 12 edgesmatlab
function karobka(A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H)
  % back face
  line([A(1) B(1)], [A(2) B(2)], [A(3) B(3)]);
  line([B(1) C(1)], [B(2) C(2)], [B(3) C(3)]);
  line([C(1) D(1)], [C(2) D(2)], [C(3) D(3)]);
  line([D(1) A(1)], [D(2) A(2)], [D(3) A(3)]);
  % struts
  line([B(1) E(1)], [B(2) E(2)], [B(3) E(3)]);
  line([A(1) F(1)], [A(2) F(2)], [A(3) F(3)]);
  line([C(1) H(1)], [C(2) H(2)], [C(3) H(3)]);
  line([D(1) G(1)], [D(2) G(2)], [D(3) G(3)]);
  % front face
  line([E(1) H(1)], [E(2) H(2)], [E(3) H(3)]);
  line([E(1) F(1)], [E(2) F(2)], [E(3) F(3)]);
  line([H(1) G(1)], [H(2) G(2)], [H(3) G(3)]);
  line([F(1) G(1)], [F(2) G(2)], [F(3) G(3)]);
end

You're given the eight vertices floating in UVW. Wire them up with the right twelve edges — back face, struts, front face. Pick a wrong pair and the strut highlights red until you fix it.

08
Animation loop

Spinning the box around Y

To animate, we loop over an angle $\theta$, build $R_y(\theta)$, transform every vertex, and redraw. Each iteration calls karobka with the rotated vertices and drawnow flushes the figure.

The pattern is identical to the single-point case — we just do the multiplication eight times per frame instead of once. That's the whole secret of rigid-body animation.

Once you can rotate eight points, you can rotate eight thousand. The matrix doesn't get any heavier.
key idea
Same rule, more points
$A'_{xyz} = R \cdot A_{uvw}$ applies to every single vertex with the same $R$. That's what "rigid" means.
Animation loopmatlab
for t = 0:0.05:2*pi
  R = [cos(t) 0 sin(t); 0 1 0; -sin(t) 0 cos(t)];
  A_xyz = R * A_uvw;  B_xyz = R * B_uvw;
  C_xyz = R * C_uvw;  D_xyz = R * D_uvw;
  E_xyz = R * E_uvw;  F_xyz = R * F_uvw;
  G_xyz = R * G_uvw;  H_xyz = R * H_uvw;
  clf; hold on; axis equal; view(130,30);
  karobka(A_xyz, B_xyz, C_xyz, D_xyz, ...
          E_xyz, F_xyz, G_xyz, H_xyz);
  drawnow;
end

  2. $R_z(\theta_2)$ — fixed
  3. $R_w(\theta_3)$ — mobile
  4. $R_y(\theta_4)$ — fixed
  5. $R_v(\theta_5)$ — mobile
  6. $R_w(\theta_6)$ — mobile

Apply the pre/post rule and obtain the composite rotation matrix by hand. Then implement the same matrix as a single function in MATLAB and verify that all three methods agree:

  1. The step-by-step pre/post chain.
  2. The single big multiplication built inside a MATLAB script.
  3. Your hand-derived matrix entered directly as a function.

All three must produce identical numerical output for the same $\theta_i$.

Doing it by hand once is how the rule stops feeling like a rule and starts feeling like algebra.
verification
Symbolic check
Use MATLAB's syms to symbolically multiply the six matrices — then compare term-by-term with your handwritten composite.

Before you sit down with pen and paper — four quick checks to make sure the pre/post rule is locked in. Miss one and the explanation tells you exactly which side of the identity the rotation should have landed on.

The box & equivalent sequences

Pick any sequence of five or six rotations $R_A, R_B, R_C, R_D, R_E, (R_F)$ — a mix of fixed and mobile axes is fine. Then:

  1. Compute its composite rotation matrix.
  2. Animate the box from class through this sequence (one motion at a time, like we did with the fly).
  3. Propose a first variant — a different sequence of motions that yields the same composite matrix. Animate it.
  4. Propose a second variant — yet another different sequence that yields the same composite matrix. Animate it.

The final orientation must be visibly identical across all three animations, even though the paths to get there are different.

Two sequences can land at the same orientation through completely different paths — that freedom is what makes redundancy possible in real robots.
hint
How variants exist at all
Adjacent fixed-frame rotations don't commute, but cleverly swapping a fixed motion for an equivalent mobile motion (with a different angle) often lets you reach the same final state.
