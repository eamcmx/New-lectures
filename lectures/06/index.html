§ 0

What this lecture covers

We take one concrete industrial robot — the Mitsubishi RV-M1, a 5-axis all-revolute manipulator — and use it as the running example to connect physical structure, kinematic description, and dynamic model.

The path is: physical skeleton → DH table → homogeneous transforms → forward kinematics → Euler–Lagrange dynamics → a MATLAB function five_dof_dynamics that returns the joint torques for any pose, velocity and acceleration. Along the way we look at where the model is fragile (frame assignment, sign conventions, units) and where it is interesting (dynamic coupling, gravity loading, Coriolis terms).

By the end of this lecture you should be able to look at an industrial arm, draw its frames, write its DH table, and predict — at least qualitatively — what torques each motor has to produce.
Learning outcomes

Relate physical structure to mathematical model; apply and interpret the DH convention on a real arm; connect kinematics to dynamics and to a MATLAB implementation; recognise the pitfalls of modeling multi-link robots.

§ 1

Physical structure and kinematic skeleton

The RV-M1 has five revolute joints. Each joint adds a degree of freedom, and each link length or offset shapes the reach, the workspace, and the dexterity of the arm.

  • Shoulder (Joint 1): base rotation that lifts the whole arm off the ground, with a vertical offset of 152 mm.
  • Arm (Joints 2–3): two long links of 250 mm and 160 mm that produce the main reach in a roughly horizontal plane.
  • Wrist (Joints 4–5): two short links of 72 mm and 107 mm that control orientation.

The "skeleton" you draw on paper is what later becomes the DH frame assignment — so any sloppiness here propagates into every transform downstream.

Think of the arm as three functional blocks: a shoulder that points the whole arm, an arm that decides where the hand is, and a wrist that decides how the hand is oriented.
Insight

DH frame assignment is not a formality. Frame placement determines every term in the forward and inverse kinematics, and every entry in the dynamic matrices. A bad offset or a flipped twist produces a model that looks plausible but is wrong everywhere.

Each parameter has a clear physical meaning: $a_n$ is the link length (it sets reach and the workspace boundary), $\alpha_n$ is the link twist (it bends the chain out of a plane), $\theta_n$ is the revolute joint variable (the actuation input), and $d_n$ is the joint offset along $z$. The two $\alpha=\pi/2$ twists at links 1 and 4 are what turn this from a planar arm into a true 3D manipulator with an orientable wrist.

A change in any DH parameter alters every transformation downstream — so treat this table as the contract between the robot and the model.
Why DH parameters?

Four numbers per link is the minimum needed to fix the geometry between consecutive frames once you accept the DH conventions ($z$ along the joint axis, $x$ along the common normal). Anything fewer is ambiguous; anything more is redundant.

§ 3

From kinematics to dynamics: the full model

Once the DH table is fixed, every link gets a homogeneous transform of the standard form

$$ {}^{n-1}\mathbf{A}_n = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_n & -\sin\theta_n\cos\alpha_n & \sin\theta_n\sin\alpha_n & a_n\cos\theta_n \\ \sin\theta_n & \cos\theta_n\cos\alpha_n & -\cos\theta_n\sin\alpha_n & a_n\sin\theta_n \\ 0 & \sin\alpha_n & \cos\alpha_n & d_n \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

The end-effector pose is then just the chain product

$${}^0\mathbf{T}_5 = {}^0\mathbf{A}_1\,{}^1\mathbf{A}_2\,{}^2\mathbf{A}_3\,{}^3\mathbf{A}_4\,{}^4\mathbf{A}_5.$$

Building the dynamics on top of this — Euler–Lagrange or recursive Newton–Euler — gives the canonical robot equation of motion

$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\dot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q}),$$

with joint vector $\mathbf{q} = [\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_3,\theta_4,\theta_5]^T$. The three terms have clean interpretations: $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is the configuration-dependent inertia, $\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ collects Coriolis and centrifugal effects, and $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})$ is the gravity torque.

The inertia matrix $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ for a 5-DOF arm is $5\times 5$ and almost never diagonal — the off-diagonal entries are exactly the dynamic coupling between joints.
Where the difficulty lives

For a serial arm, $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$ is highly configuration-dependent and typically full. Every off-diagonal entry is a coupling inertia, meaning that accelerating one joint generically produces reaction torques at all the others.

§ 4

MATLAB implementation: five_dof_dynamics

The companion MATLAB function five_dof_dynamics(vv, veloc, acc, unit) packages everything above into one call. Given a posture, a velocity vector and an acceleration vector, it returns the five joint torques required to produce that motion.

Internally it computes the forward kinematics from the DH table, propagates link velocities and accelerations recursively, evaluates the kinetic and potential energy of each link, assembles $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})$, $\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})$ and $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})$, and finally produces $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ for the input trajectory point.

A good first sanity check is gravity compensation — all velocities and accelerations zero, so the torques you get back are exactly what the motors must hold to keep the arm static against gravity in that pose.

Run gravity-only first. If those torques don't make physical sense — small at the singular vertical pose, big at the horizontal stretched pose — something in the model is wrong before you ever add motion.
Reading the output

Each element of the returned torque vector is the actuator effort at one joint. Sign follows the right-hand rule consistent with the DH frame assignment.

Common mistakes

Mixing degrees and radians in vv — use the unit argument. Forgetting that veloc and acc are always in rad/s and rad/s² regardless of unit. Misreading sign conventions when the robot "moves the wrong way".

Gravity compensation: torques to hold a static posematlab
% Joint positions (degrees), velocities (rad/s), accelerations (rad/s^2)
vv    = [0; 45; 30; 0; 0];   % shoulder, arm, elbow, wrist, wrist
veloc = zeros(5,1);          % stationary
acc   = zeros(5,1);          % no acceleration
unit  = 'deg';               % input in degrees

% Torques required to hold the arm in this posture against gravity
torque = five_dof_dynamics(vv, veloc, acc, unit);

disp('Joint torques required (N*m):');
disp(torque);

§ 5

Modifying and extending the model

The MATLAB function is built around the RV-M1 numbers, but the structure is generic. There are two natural ways to extend it:

  • Add a payload. Increase the mass of the last link m5, or equivalently add the payload weight to the gravity computation for joint 5. The gravity term $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})$ should grow most at joints carrying the moment arm — typically 2 and 3.
  • Adapt to another robot. Replace the link lengths, masses, centers of mass, and inertia matrices at the top of the function with the values for the new arm, and make sure they are expressed in frames consistent with the new DH assignment.

The key implementation rule is consistency: link masses, inertias, and centers of mass must live in the same frames as those produced by the DH transforms. The standard validation recipe is to test with all joints at zero (checks the gravity terms), then drive sinusoidal trajectories (excites Coriolis and coupling), then add payloads and watch how the torque profile responds.

Frame consistency is non-negotiable

If the inertia tensor of a link is expressed in a different frame from the one DH produces for that link, every term it feeds into the dynamics will be wrong — even though every individual matrix looks fine on its own.

Picker What-if · pick a model change, see what it costs you

Pick a single modification — longer upper arm, added payload, locked elbow, or lighter wrist — and the panel reports the dominant effect on the workspace, the gravity torque, and which joint becomes the bottleneck. The goal is to read the dynamics qualitatively before you compute anything.

§ 6

Discussion: what the model teaches us

Once the dynamic model is in your hands, it starts to say things about the robot:

  • Frame assignment impacts everything. Small changes in DH offsets or twists can dramatically change the dynamic model and the resulting control behaviour.
  • Coupling effects are real. On the RV-M1, moving joint 2 alone still produces velocities and torques at the other joints because of mass/inertia coupling.
  • Gravity loading peaks at horizontal postures. When the arm is stretched out, multiple joints have to cooperate to hold up the arm plus payload.
  • Coriolis and centrifugal terms are nonlinear and counterintuitive. Simultaneous motion of shoulder and elbow can produce velocity-dependent torques at the wrist that you would not have guessed from static reasoning.
  • Algorithmic efficiency matters for real-time control. Vectorising the MATLAB code and pre-computing symbolic expressions for the dynamic terms is best practice if you want this to run in a controller loop.

A recurring industrial design pattern is also worth noticing: many industrial robots arrange the wrist so all three wrist axes intersect at a single point. This decouples position and orientation in the inverse-kinematics problem, which is the single biggest practical win in arm design.

The dynamic model is not just simulation glue — it is the thing that lets you explain why a real arm sags, why it overshoots in one direction more than another, and why some postures are dangerous.

1 · Sketch the zero configuration

Using the RV-M1 DH table from §2, sketch the arm when $\theta_1=\theta_2=\theta_3=\theta_4=\theta_5=0$. Mark each frame's origin and its $z$-axis, and locate the end-effector position in millimetres relative to the base.

Self-check after you've tried
  • Frame 0 sits at the base, frame 1 is lifted by $d_1=152$ mm along $z_0$.
  • At zero angles the two long links (250 mm and 160 mm) extend along a single straight line.
  • Your end-effector $x$-coordinate should be around $250+160+72+107$ mm projected along the link direction; $z$ should include the 152 mm shoulder offset.
Show worked solution

At zero angles, each individual transform reduces dramatically because $\cos\theta_n=1$ and $\sin\theta_n=0$:

$${}^{n-1}\mathbf{A}_n\big|_{\theta_n=0} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & a_n \\ 0 & \cos\alpha_n & -\sin\alpha_n & 0 \\ 0 & \sin\alpha_n & \cos\alpha_n & d_n \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Applying this to each link with the table values gives translations $(0,0,152)$, $(250,0,0)$, $(160,0,0)$, $(72,0,0)$, $(107,0,0)$ in their local frames, with $\alpha_1=\alpha_4=\pi/2$ rotating the subsequent frames out of the previous link's plane.

Multiplying out, the end-effector lies on the straight stretched arm, displaced by $a_2+a_3+a_4+a_5 = 250+160+72+107 = 589$ mm from the shoulder along the arm's projection, sitting at height $d_1 = 152$ mm above the base. So in the base frame the end-effector position is approximately

$$\boxed{\;p_{ee} \approx (589,\,0,\,152)\;\text{mm}\;}$$

with its precise direction set by how the two $\alpha=\pi/2$ twists fold the wrist out of the arm plane.

2 · Effect of doubling $a_2$

Predict what happens to the workspace if the upper-arm length $a_2$ is doubled from 250 mm to 500 mm. Sketch the new reachable region in the vertical plane through joints 2 and 3, and reason about how payload capacity and singularity positions change.

Self-check after you've tried
  • The outer reach should grow but the inner "dead zone" near the shoulder should also change.
  • The maximum gravity torque at joint 2 should increase (longer moment arm).
  • Singular configurations occur where the arm and forearm are colinear — those geometric loci stay the same in shape but move outward in radius.
Show worked solution

The planar reach of joints 2 and 3 is annular: outer radius $a_2+a_3$, inner radius $|a_2-a_3|$. With the original $(a_2,a_3)=(250,160)$ mm we get an annulus with outer radius $410$ mm and inner radius $90$ mm. Doubling $a_2$ to $500$ mm gives outer radius $660$ mm and inner radius $340$ mm — the workspace grows outward but the unreachable hole near the shoulder grows even faster.

Payload effect. The static gravity torque at joint 2 due to link 2 scales like $m_2 g \cdot (a_2/2)$, and the torque it must support from the rest of the arm scales like $(m_3 + m_4 + m_5)g \cdot a_2$ in the worst-case horizontal stretched pose. So doubling $a_2$ roughly doubles the worst-case gravity torque at joint 2, which means either bigger motors or a smaller payload allowance.

Singularities. The elbow singularity at $\theta_3 = 0$ (arm fully stretched) and the inner-boundary singularity at $\theta_3 = \pi$ (arm folded back) are unchanged in angle, but the cartesian locus where they occur moves outward to radii $660$ mm and $340$ mm respectively. So the geometry of the singularities is preserved; their position in the workspace shifts.

$$\boxed{\;\text{Outer reach}\,410\,\text{mm} \to 660\,\text{mm};\ \text{inner hole}\,90\,\text{mm} \to 340\,\text{mm};\ \tau_{2,\text{grav}}\ \text{roughly doubles}.\;}$$

3 · Reduced manipulator with joint 3 locked

Suppose joint 3 is locked at some fixed angle $\theta_3 = \theta_3^\star$. Describe the resulting reduced manipulator: how many degrees of freedom does it have, and what is the shape of its reachable workspace?

Self-check after you've tried
  • DOF count is the original count minus one.
  • Locking joint 3 fuses links 2 and 3 into a single rigid "effective link" of fixed geometry.
  • The reduced workspace is a surface, not a volume, when DOF $<$ 6 in task space — describe it carefully.
Show worked solution

Locking joint 3 freezes the angle between links 2 and 3. The two links and their geometry collapse into one effective link whose end is at a fixed offset from frame 2 that depends on $\theta_3^\star$. We now have a 4-DOF arm: $\theta_1$ (shoulder rotation about the vertical axis), $\theta_2$ (rotation of the fused arm about the shoulder), and $\theta_4,\theta_5$ (wrist).

The position of the wrist centre is then controlled by only two joints, $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$. Joint 1 sweeps the arm around the vertical; joint 2 rotates the fused arm in a vertical plane that joint 1 sets. The wrist centre therefore lies on a sphere of radius $r^\star$ centred at the shoulder, where $r^\star$ is the distance from the shoulder to the wrist centre with the elbow fixed at $\theta_3^\star$. For our table this is

$$r^\star = \sqrt{a_2^2 + a_3^2 + 2 a_2 a_3 \cos\theta_3^\star}.$$

So the reachable wrist positions form a spherical shell (a 2D surface in 3-space) of radius $r^\star$ centred at the shoulder, instead of the full 3D annular volume of the original 5-DOF arm. Orientation is still controlled by the wrist joints $\theta_4,\theta_5$, so the wrist can rotate, but it can no longer be moved off that sphere.

$$\boxed{\;\text{4-DOF arm; wrist centre confined to a sphere of radius }\, r^\star = \sqrt{a_2^2 + a_3^2 + 2 a_2 a_3 \cos\theta_3^\star}\;\text{about the shoulder.}\;}$$

4 · Shape of the gravity-torque curve at joint 2

Holding all other joints at zero, predict the shape of the gravity torque required at joint 2 as $\theta_2$ sweeps from $-90°$ to $+90°$. Sketch the curve and explain its shape physically. (You can verify by running five_dof_dynamics(vv, zeros(5,1), zeros(5,1), 'deg') for a sweep of vv = [0; theta2; 0; 0; 0].)

Self-check after you've tried
  • At $\theta_2 = 0$ (arm horizontal) the gravity torque should be near its maximum.
  • At $\theta_2 = \pm 90°$ (arm vertical) it should drop to roughly zero.
  • The curve should look like a cosine in $\theta_2$.
Show worked solution

With $\theta_3=\theta_4=\theta_5=0$, links 2 through 5 are effectively a single rigid extension of total length $L_{eq}=a_2+a_3+a_4+a_5$ and total mass $M$, with centre of mass at some distance $r_{cm}$ along that extension from the joint-2 axis. The gravitational torque about joint 2 is

$$\tau_{2,\text{grav}}(\theta_2) = M g\, r_{cm}\cos\theta_2,$$

because the moment arm of the centre of mass about the horizontal joint-2 axis equals $r_{cm}\cos\theta_2$ when $\theta_2$ is measured from the horizontal. This gives a cosine-shaped curve: maximum at $\theta_2 = 0$ (arm fully horizontal, maximum lever arm), passing through zero at $\theta_2 = \pm 90°$ (arm pointing straight up or straight down, no lever arm), and symmetric about $\theta_2=0$.

Physically: gravity always pulls down with the same force, but only the component perpendicular to the arm produces a torque about the shoulder. That component goes as $\cos\theta_2$.

$$\boxed{\;\tau_{2,\text{grav}}(\theta_2) = M g\, r_{cm}\cos\theta_2 \;}$$

This is also why the worst loading for the joint-2 motor on a real industrial arm is at horizontal stretched poses, not at the extremes of travel.
