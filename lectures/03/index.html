Kinematics and Energies for a 2-Link Planar Robot Arm

From transformation matrices to the Lagrangian — a full worked derivation for tip masses $m_1$ and $m_2$.

Robotics / Robot Dynamics Module 2 Worked derivation
Today we slow down and walk every line of a derivation you'll lean on again and again — the kinematics and energies of a two-link planar arm. We'll start with the homogeneous transforms, read off positions, differentiate to get velocities, square them to get $v^2$, and then assemble the kinetic and potential energies into a Lagrangian. Every step is honest algebra: no skipped lines, no hand-waving. By the end you'll have the $L = T - V$ that Module 3 needs for the Euler–Lagrange equations.

Section 0

What this lecture covers

We work through a complete derivation for a planar two-link manipulator carrying tip masses $m_1$ (at the end of link 1) and $m_2$ (at the end of link 2). The pipeline is fixed: transforms → positions → velocities → $v^2$ → kinetic energy $T$ → potential energy $V$ → Lagrangian $L = T - V$.

Nothing here is new physics — it's the careful bookkeeping that lets us hand a clean $L$ to the Euler–Lagrange machinery in Module 3.

If you can do this one cleanly, you can do every serial-chain Lagrangian you'll ever meet — the rest is just more links.

Setup

Planar 2-link arm. Link lengths $L_1, L_2$. Joint angles $\theta_1, \theta_2$. Point masses $m_1, m_2$ at the distal end of each link. Gravity $g$ acts in $-y$.

Section 1

Homogeneous transformation matrices

Each link contributes a planar rigid-body transform — a rotation by its joint angle and a translation along its own length. Stacking these gives us the pose of every point on the arm relative to the base frame.

The transform from the base to the end of link 1:

$$T_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_1 & -\sin\theta_1 & L_1\cos\theta_1 \\ \sin\theta_1 & \cos\theta_1 & L_1\sin\theta_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

The transform from the base to the end of link 2 (i.e. to $m_2$):

$$T_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ \sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
The third column is the part we actually care about — it's where the mass lives.

Why $\theta_1+\theta_2$?

Link 2's orientation in the world frame is the sum of the two joint angles because link 2's frame is mounted on link 1's frame — rotations compose by addition in 2D.

Section 2

Positions of the tip masses

Read the $(x, y)$ coordinates straight off the third column of each transform.

For $m_1$ (end of link 1):

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &= L_1\cos\theta_1 \\ y_1 &= L_1\sin\theta_1 \end{aligned}$$

For $m_2$ (end of link 2):

$$\begin{aligned} x_2 &= L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ y_2 &= L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{aligned}$$

Now you pick a joint configuration and read the resulting tip positions straight off the formulas. The arm redraws, and the $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ values update so you can see how the position equations from Section 2 behave.

Section 3

Velocities by the chain rule

Differentiate the position equations with respect to time. Every $\theta_i$ becomes a $\dot{\theta}_i$ on the way out.

For $m_1$:

$$\begin{aligned} \dot{x}_1 &= -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 \\ \dot{y}_1 &= \;\;\,L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 \end{aligned}$$

For $m_2$:

$$\begin{aligned} \dot{x}_2 &= -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 - L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2) \\ \dot{y}_2 &= \;\;\,L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\,(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2) \end{aligned}$$
Notice the $(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)$ factor — link 2's angular velocity in the world is the sum.

Common slip

When differentiating $\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$, the chain rule pulls out $(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)$, not just $\dot{\theta}_2$. Forgetting the first term is the most common mistake in this whole derivation.

Now we watch the chain rule act on $x_2 = L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$ one symbol at a time. Each step you advance, the animator highlights a sub-expression, applies $\frac{d}{dt}$ to it, and writes the result underneath — so by the end you see exactly where every $\dot{\theta}_i$ came from and why the $(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)$ factor appears.

Section 4

Computing $v^2$ for each mass

Square the velocity components and add. The Pythagorean identity does most of the cleanup.

For $m_1$ — the easy one:

$$v_1^2 = \dot{x}_1^2 + \dot{y}_1^2 = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2(\sin^2\theta_1 + \cos^2\theta_1) = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2$$

For $m_2$ — expand both squares, collect, and use two trig identities:

$$\begin{aligned} v_2^2 &= L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2\,(\sin^2\theta_1+\cos^2\theta_1) \\ &\quad + L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2\,(\sin^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)+\cos^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)) \\ &\quad + 2L_1L_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)\,[\sin\theta_1\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)+\cos\theta_1\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)] \end{aligned}$$

Using $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$ and the angle-difference identity $\cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta = \cos(\alpha - \beta)$, the cross term collapses to $\cos\theta_2$:

$$\boxed{\,v_2^2 = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)\,}$$
Two identities — Pythagoras and angle-difference — do all the real work here.

Identity used

$\cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta = \cos(\alpha - \beta)$. With $\alpha = \theta_1+\theta_2$ and $\beta = \theta_1$, the difference is $\theta_2$.

Now you collapse the messy cross term yourself. You start with the expanded $v_2^2$ from Section 4 and drag in the two identities — Pythagorean and angle-difference — onto the right sub-expressions to peel it down to the boxed form. The widget refuses substitutions whose $\alpha,\beta$ don't actually match the bracket you're targeting, so the algebra has to be honest.

Section 5

Total kinetic energy $T$

Sum $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ over both masses:

$$T = \tfrac{1}{2}m_1 v_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2 v_2^2$$

Substituting the simplified expressions:

$$T = \tfrac{1}{2}m_1 L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2\Big[L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)\Big]$$

This is the kinetic energy in generalised coordinates $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ — exactly the form the Euler–Lagrange equations need.

Why this form matters

Written this way, $T$ depends only on the joint angles and their rates — no Cartesian coordinates to chase. That's the whole point of going through generalised coordinates.

Section 6

Potential energy $V$

With $y$ pointing up and gravity $g$ acting downward, the potential energy of each mass is $mgy$:

$$V = m_1 g\,y_1 + m_2 g\,y_2$$

Substituting the height expressions from Section 2:

$$V = m_1 g L_1\sin\theta_1 + m_2 g\,[L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)]$$
Only the $y$-coordinates show up — gravity doesn't care how fast you swing horizontally.
Section 7

Assembling the Lagrangian

The Lagrangian is the difference of kinetic and potential energy:

$$L = T - V$$

Writing it out in full:

$$\begin{aligned} L = \;& \tfrac{1}{2}m_1 L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 \\ & + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2\Big[L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)\Big] \\ & - m_1 g L_1\sin\theta_1 - m_2 g\,[L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)] \end{aligned}$$

This is what you feed into the Euler–Lagrange equations $\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\theta}_i} - \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_i} = \tau_i$ to extract the equations of motion. That's Module 3.

Hold onto this $L$ — we'll come back to it the moment we start Module 3.

What you've built

A complete generalised-coordinate description of a two-link arm with tip masses — positions, velocities, energies, and the Lagrangian — derived without skipping a single line.

Practice — try these on your own

Small activities to test your grip on today's ideas. Try each one first, then reveal the worked solution to compare.

1 · Re-derive $v_1^2$ from scratch

Starting from the position equations $x_1 = L_1\cos\theta_1$, $y_1 = L_1\sin\theta_1$, differentiate to get $\dot{x}_1, \dot{y}_1$ and then compute $v_1^2 = \dot{x}_1^2 + \dot{y}_1^2$. Show that the answer reduces to a single term.
Self-check after you've tried
  • Your answer should have no trig functions in it.
  • The only variables left should be $L_1$ and $\dot{\theta}_1$.
  • The Pythagorean identity $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$ must appear in the simplification.
Show worked solution

Differentiating with respect to time:

$$\dot{x}_1 = -L_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1, \qquad \dot{y}_1 = L_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1$$

Square each:

$$\dot{x}_1^2 = L_1^2\sin^2\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1^2, \qquad \dot{y}_1^2 = L_1^2\cos^2\theta_1\,\dot{\theta}_1^2$$

Add:

$$v_1^2 = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2\,(\sin^2\theta_1 + \cos^2\theta_1) = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2$$ $$\boxed{v_1^2 = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2}$$

The trig dependence vanishes — kinetic energy of a point on a rotating link doesn't care where in its circle it currently sits, only how fast it sweeps.

2 · Verify the cross-term simplification in $v_2^2$

The expansion of $v_2^2$ contains the bracket $[\sin\theta_1\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) + \cos\theta_1\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)]$. Show by direct application of the angle-difference identity that this bracket equals $\cos\theta_2$.
Self-check after you've tried
  • You should be using $\cos(\alpha - \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta$.
  • The identity is matched right-hand side to left (you're collapsing a sum into a single cosine).
  • Check by plugging $\theta_1 = 0$: the bracket becomes $0 + \cos\theta_2 = \cos\theta_2$, which matches.
Show worked solution

The angle-difference identity for cosine states:

$$\cos(\alpha - \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta$$

Set $\alpha = \theta_1 + \theta_2$ and $\beta = \theta_1$. The right-hand side becomes

$$\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\cos\theta_1 + \sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\sin\theta_1$$

which is exactly the bracket in question (just with the terms in the other order). Therefore

$$\sin\theta_1\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) + \cos\theta_1\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) = \cos\big((\theta_1+\theta_2) - \theta_1\big) = \cos\theta_2$$ $$\boxed{\;\text{bracket} = \cos\theta_2\;}$$

That single substitution is what turns the messy cross term into the clean $2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)$.

3 · Sanity-check at $\theta_2 = 0$

If $\theta_2 = 0$, the two links are colinear and the arm behaves like a single rigid link of length $L_1 + L_2$ rotating with combined angular rate $\dot{\theta}_1 + \dot{\theta}_2$? Actually, be careful — at $\theta_2 = 0$ link 2 only rotates with rate $\dot{\theta}_1$ when $\dot{\theta}_2 = 0$ too. Set $\theta_2 = 0$ and $\dot{\theta}_2 = 0$ in the formula for $v_2^2$ and confirm that you recover $v_2^2 = (L_1 + L_2)^2 \dot{\theta}_1^2$.
Self-check after you've tried
  • With $\theta_2 = \dot{\theta}_2 = 0$, $\cos\theta_2 = 1$.
  • The three terms should combine via the algebraic identity $a^2 + b^2 + 2ab = (a+b)^2$.
  • The final answer must factor as a perfect square of $(L_1 + L_2)\,\dot{\theta}_1$.
Show worked solution

Start from

$$v_2^2 = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1+\dot{\theta}_2)$$

Substitute $\theta_2 = 0$ so $\cos\theta_2 = 1$, and $\dot{\theta}_2 = 0$:

$$v_2^2 = L_1^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + L_2^2\dot{\theta}_1^2 + 2L_1L_2\,\dot{\theta}_1^2 = (L_1^2 + 2L_1L_2 + L_2^2)\,\dot{\theta}_1^2$$

The coefficient is a perfect square:

$$\boxed{v_2^2 = (L_1 + L_2)^2\,\dot{\theta}_1^2}$$

This is exactly the squared tangential speed of a point at radius $L_1 + L_2$ rotating about the base at rate $\dot{\theta}_1$ — a straight-arm rotation. The formula passes the sanity check.

4 · Compute $V$ at the 'arm-straight-up' pose

Find the total potential energy $V$ when $\theta_1 = \pi/2$ and $\theta_2 = 0$ (both links pointing straight up). Express your answer in terms of $m_1, m_2, g, L_1, L_2$.
Self-check after you've tried
  • $\sin(\pi/2) = 1$ and $\sin(\pi/2 + 0) = 1$.
  • The answer should be a sum of two simple $mgh$ terms with $m_1$ at height $L_1$ and $m_2$ at height $L_1 + L_2$.
  • No trig should remain.
Show worked solution

From Section 6:

$$V = m_1 g L_1 \sin\theta_1 + m_2 g\,[L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)]$$

Plug in $\theta_1 = \pi/2$, $\theta_2 = 0$. Then $\sin\theta_1 = 1$ and $\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) = \sin(\pi/2) = 1$.

$$V = m_1 g L_1 (1) + m_2 g\,[L_1(1) + L_2(1)] = m_1 g L_1 + m_2 g (L_1 + L_2)$$ $$\boxed{V = m_1 g L_1 + m_2 g (L_1 + L_2)}$$

This is exactly the textbook $\sum m_i g h_i$ for two point masses at heights $L_1$ and $L_1 + L_2$ — the formula collapses to the obvious answer for the arm-vertical pose.
